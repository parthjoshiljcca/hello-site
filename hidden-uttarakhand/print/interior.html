Preface
Preface

I grew up in Ahmedabad, where the tallest thing for miles is a flyover, and the only mountain anyone talks about is the heap of shoes outside the mandir on Janmashtami.

So I came to the Himalaya the way most of us from the plains do: as a tourist. I knew the postcard Uttarakhand. Char Dham. Snow peaks. Maggi at 3,000 metres. A sunrise you photograph instead of watching.

What I didn’t know was that the postcard has a back side.

On the back side, a god of justice receives handwritten petitions and answers them with bells. A village refuses to forgive Hanuman. A goddess is walked to her husband’s home once every twelve years behind a four-horned ram — and this year, two committees couldn’t agree on when. A lake at 5,000 metres keeps the bones of strangers from the other side of the world. A mountain keeps a nuclear device that two governments would rather you forgot. And on certain nights, in certain meadows, you are told not to answer if someone calls your name.

None of this is hidden, exactly. It is simply unheard. It lives in the songs of the jagariya, in the warnings of grandmothers, in the stories a driver tells on a hairpin bend at midnight to keep himself awake. Most of it has never been written down in English. Some of it is slowly not being sung at all, as the young leave the hills for the plains and the drums go quiet in empty villages.

This book is my attempt to listen before that happens.

I am not a historian, a folklorist or a priest. I am a professor who teaches young people how machines think, a developer who writes code while the house is asleep, and a storyteller who has never been able to hear a legend without wanting to pass it on. And under all three, I am a believer — the kind who lights a diya every evening and then sits beside it asking why, until the flame gets tired of him.

So I went to the mountains with my questions. The mountains, it turns out, ask better ones.

This book is what they let me carry home.

Parth
Ahmedabad, 2026

My name is Parth. I’m a professor — twelve years in classrooms, teaching computers to think and students to question everything, including me. Somewhere between the code and the diya, I became a man who has to know why.

In October 2026, I went to Uttarakhand to “relax.” I took two friends, one driver, three sweaters, and a notebook for writing down the legends I heard.

I came back without the notebook, with a different notebook, a police statement, a YouTube channel that will not stop tagging me, and the phone number of a man who wanted something buried under the snow of Nanda Devi badly enough to chase us across an entire state.

This book is what happened in between.

Three rules before we begin:

  • Everything marked as fact is fact. Dates, court cases, DNA studies, a nuclear device the CIA really did lose on a Himalayan peak — all real, all sourced at the back.
  • Everything marked as legend is legend. I don’t mock it. When the drums start, the jokes stop. I’ll tell you when.
  • Everything else is a story. The chase, the villain, the song. Read the honest part below before you call the police on anyone.

The honest part (no jokes, I promise)

On facts. Every date, trial, expedition and scientific finding in this book comes from a published source, listed at the back. Where sources disagree, I say so. Where I couldn’t confirm something, I’ve told you I couldn’t.

On legends. The folklore of Uttarakhand is not one story but thousands, and it changes from valley to valley, sometimes from house to house. The versions here are the ones I heard or read, credited to the people who tell them. If your grandmother tells it differently, she is not wrong. She is simply from the next valley.

On faith. Temples, Jagar, Pandav Lila, the Raj Jat — these are living worship, not tourist shows. I laugh at myself constantly in this book. I do not laugh at anyone’s gods. During rituals, the jokes stop.

On people. Shaoni and Kshitij are real, and appear as themselves. Gabbar, Ghostbuster Golu and the local storytellers are fictional, built from many people I met and many stories I heard; any resemblance to a real driver who also names his car Basanti is a coincidence I would love to hear about.

On fiction. The 1965 Nanda Devi expedition is real and its device has never been found. Everything else about it in this book is invented: Rohan Sehgal, his company and crew, the chase, and the shepherd song about Nanda ki garam shila, the goddess’s warm stone. No real family sings that song. Deepak and Gaurav Rawat, Bachi Ram ji, Kamla Devi, Dhana ji, Hira, and every family that guards a piece of the story are invented too. No real company is behind this. If you go looking for the device, please don’t.

On real tragedies. The hills have lost many people — to floods, landslides, storms and forests that do not give back what they take. Where families are still waiting for answers, I have left their stories out. Folklore is old enough to be told. Grief this fresh belongs to the people living it.

On safety. Please don’t trek alone, don’t leave camp at night, and listen to locals when they tell you where not to go. The legends about fairies and voices in the forest have kept people alive for centuries. They’re good advice in a very old costume.

Introduction
Introduction

What Happened on Nanda Devi, 1965

Everything in this section is true. No jokes. No fiction. I promise you’ll want both later.

In October 1964, China tested its first atomic bomb at Lop Nur, in the deserts of Xinjiang, across the Tibetan plateau from India. Two years earlier, India and China had fought a war in these same mountains. Washington wanted to know how far Beijing’s missiles could reach. New Delhi wanted to know the same thing, and urgently.

The idea that followed sounds like a film. Put a listening station on top of a Himalayan peak, high enough to look over the plateau and catch the radio signals from Chinese missile tests. Power it with something that would keep running for years without anyone climbing up to change a battery.

That something was a SNAP-19C — a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, a device that turns the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity. Seven plutonium capsules sat inside it. The peak chosen was Nanda Devi, at 7,817 metres the highest mountain entirely within India, and to the people of Garhwal and Kumaon, the home of their goddess.

The mission was a secret partnership between the American CIA and India’s Intelligence Bureau. Its Indian leader was Captain M.S. Kohli, who only months earlier had led India’s first successful Everest expedition. He brought some of his Everest climbers with him, along with Sherpas and a small team of American mountaineers. In the summer of 1965 they trained together on Denali in Alaska. By mid-September they were at Nanda Devi base camp, many of them flown in by helicopter so as to attract as little attention as possible.

They climbed late in the season, with the good weather already gone. They established camps one above another. The plan was for Sherpas to carry the equipment from Camp IV to the summit, about 300 metres higher, and for a team of two Indian and two American climbers to follow and assemble it.

At Camp IV, a blizzard hit.

On 16 October 1965, Kohli made the call to turn back. The generator — antenna, transceivers and the plutonium-powered unit — was anchored on the slope near the camp, and the climbers came down. There would be no more climbing that season.

In May 1966, a team went back up to Camp IV to retrieve it.

It was gone.

Not buried where they’d left it. Not anywhere they could see. Later searches, including American climbers sweeping the slopes with neutron detectors, found no trace of it. The best guess was that an avalanche or landslide had carried it down the mountain, into a glacier. Some believe the generator’s own heat melted the ice around it and let it sink deeper. One writer has claimed it may have been quietly recovered before the search team ever arrived. No one has ever proved what happened.

The agencies didn’t give up on the idea. In 1967, a similar device was installed on Nanda Kot, a neighbouring peak. It worked for a few months. When a team climbed up in 1968 to retrieve it, they found it had sunk into the snow — sitting at the bottom of a hollow it had melted for itself, eight feet in every direction. Still warm.

Remember that detail. Snow melting around something hot, high on a mountain, where nothing should be hot at all.

The whole operation stayed secret for more than a decade, until an American magazine, Outside, broke the story in April 1978. Questions were asked in the US Congress and in India’s Parliament, and Prime Minister Morarji Desai gave the Lok Sabha a formal account. A scientific committee recommended regular monitoring of the air, water and soil around Nanda Devi for radioactivity.

The Nanda Devi device has never been found.

The glaciers that feed the Rishiganga, and through it the Alaknanda and the Ganga, keep it somewhere. Every year, those glaciers are a little smaller. Every time a flood comes down the valley — as one did, catastrophically, in February 2021 — people in the villages below ask the same question again.

That’s the true story. It’s all in the sources at the back of this book.

What follows is a story too. Some of it happened. Some of it is mine. But I want you to start the way I did, standing in the cold under Nanda Devi, knowing one thing for certain:

Somewhere on that mountain, something is still warm.

Prologue
Prologue

A page torn from the back of a stranger’s notebook. Written in pencil. Some words are shaky. The author asks that you not judge his handwriting under the circumstances.

3:12 a.m. Khaliya Top, above Munsiyari. Approximately 3,500 metres above sea level and about four metres from a very bad decision.

The fire has gone down to orange. Shaoni is asleep in the tent with her headlamp still on, because she trusts nothing, including the dark. Kshitij is sitting next to me, wrapped in two blankets and one of my sweaters, which I did not lend him.

We are both awake because of what the old woman said at dinner.

Up here, after dark, if the forest calls your name — you don’t answer. You don’t turn round. You don’t even say “who’s there.” You let it call.

I am a professor. I have two degrees. I am also a believer — I light a diya every evening, and I have never once passed a mandir without folding my hands. So I honestly don’t know what I believe about fairies who live in meadows and take people who reply to them.

I am also not answering anything tonight. These two facts sit side by side in my head like students who refuse to share a bench.

The wind stopped about ten minutes ago. That’s the part nobody warns you about. Not the noise. The silence after it.

And then, from somewhere past the edge of the firelight, out where the meadow drops into the forest, a voice said:

“Kshi-tij…”

Long. Soft. Like someone calling a child in for dinner.

Kshitij did not move. I did not move. I am writing this without moving, which I would like noted as a technical achievement.

Kshitij’s lips have gone the colour of a low-battery warning. He is looking at me the way I look at code that runs perfectly on the first try: certain that something terrible is about to happen.

Here is what I know, as a man of science:

  1. Sound carries strangely in mountains.
  2. Shepherds live nearby.
  3. Nobody up here knows Kshitij’s name except the five of us around this fire and an old woman who went to bed at nine.

Point 3 is ruining points 1 and 2.

The voice is calling again. Closer, I think. Though, see point 1.

If you are reading this, reader, then either I came back down the mountain, or someone found this page and thought it was worth publishing, in which case: please correct my grammar, and please tell my mother I wore the sweater.

The voice has stopped saying Kshitij.

It is saying Parth.

I’m not going to answer.

But I think, before I find out what’s out there, I should tell you how a perfectly sensible professor from Ahmedabad ended up four metres from the forest at three in the morning, holding his breath and a pencil.

It started, like most disasters in my life, with a spreadsheet.

Chapter One

Room Locked, Lady Dead, Professor Hungry

Day 1·Mussoorie

The first lie of the trip was Shaoni’s, and she told it at 5:40 in the morning, at Ahmedabad airport, standing under the departures board with a printed itinerary thick enough to stop a bullet.

“Bas relax karenge,” she said. She said it to me, but she was looking at her brother, the way you look at a dog that has seen a squirrel. “No adventure. No drama. Mountains, chai, sleep.”

Kshitij didn’t look up. He was sitting on his suitcase with his diary open on his knee, the fat, rubber-banded diary he has carried everywhere since he was twelve, and he was already writing.

“Day zero,” he read aloud, in the flat voice of a court reporter. “Five-forty a.m. Didi says no drama.” He underlined something twice. “Noted. For future evidence.”

“Kshitij.” Shaoni tapped the itinerary against his head. “Page one. Read.”

He read it. Then he turned to me. “Bhai, page one pe ek column hai. Risk. Har row mein likha hai Low.”

“Kyunki risk low hai,” said Shaoni.

“Har row mein,” Kshitij repeated, to me, not to her. “Forty-seven rows. Kisi bhi cheez ka risk Medium nahi hai. Kedarnath ka bhi nahi.”

I want you to understand who these two are before the mountains get hold of them, so here is the shortest possible version.

Shaoni has been my friend for so many years that we’ve stopped being polite to each other, which in India is the highest form of love. She is the only person I know who reads the terms and conditions and then argues with them. When something frightens her, she turns it into a numbered list, and by point four it’s usually just a problem, and problems she can fix. She calls this logic. Everyone else calls it courage. She would rather die than agree.

Kshitij, her younger brother, is the quietest person who has ever made me laugh. He doesn’t tell jokes. He writes things down, and then reads them out at exactly the wrong moment.

As for me: I’m a professor, a developer and a storyteller, in roughly that order before lunch and the reverse order after dinner. I light a diya every evening. I fold my hands at every mandir I pass. And when I’m nervous, I make jokes. The more nervous, the better the jokes. Keep an eye on that. On this trip, it was the only instrument that never failed.

“Aur ghosts?” I asked Shaoni, because someone had to.

She gave me the look she gives a theory with a hole in it. “Parth. Bhoot ka koi data nahi hai. Tum professor ho. Behave.”

Reader, I would like the record to show that I tried.

At Dehradun airport, a man in a grey jacket and a black Garhwali topi was leaning against a white SUV with his arms folded, watching the arrivals gate like a customs officer who had seen every kind of tourist and approved of none of them. On the back windshield, in pink vinyl letters, someone had stuck one word.

BASANTI.

I walked up to him with my best professor smile. “Taxi? Mussoorie?”

“Mussoorie,” he agreed. His eyes went over the three of us: Shaoni’s itinerary, Kshitij’s diary, my three sweaters. “Phir?”

“Phir poora Uttarakhand. Teen hafte. Mandir, pahad, kahaniyan.” I paused. “Aap free ho?”

He looked at me for a long, uncomfortable moment, a man deciding whether I was a customer or a problem. Then he patted the bonnet of the car, gently, the way you’d pat an old horse.

“Basanti ne isse bure log dekhe hain,” he said. He held out a hand the size of a dinner plate. “Gabbar Singh Negi. Fixed rate. Bhooton ka extra.”

I shook it. I hired a man named Gabbar to drive a car named Basanti. I did it on purpose. I regret nothing.

“Gabbar Singh?” said Kshitij, pen already moving. “Asli naam?”

“Asli naam.” Gabbar opened the boot and threw Shaoni’s suitcase in as if it had insulted him. “Mere papa bus conductor the, Haldwani mein. Ek sardi mein Sholay unnees baar dekhi. Agle basant mein main paida hua.” He slammed the boot. “Hero ka naam toh sab rakhte hain, unhone kaha.”

“Aur Basanti?” I asked.

“Pachaas saal se har policewala mujhse poochta hai, kitne aadmi the.” He opened the driver’s door. “Toh jab apni gaadi li, naam rakh diya Basanti. Ab koi poochta hai, toh main kehta hoon: Basanti se poocho.”

Shaoni laughed, which she almost never does at strangers. That was the moment, I think, that Gabbar decided we were customers and not a problem.

The road to Mussoorie climbs as if it’s late for something. Around the fourteenth hairpin, Kshitij stopped talking, which is how you know a road is serious. Around the twentieth, Gabbar started.

He reached across me, opened the glovebox and dropped a laminated card in my lap without taking his eyes off the bend.

Mussoorie — fixed. Char Dham — fixed. Bhoot ki kahani — Rs 100 extra. Asli bhoot ki kahani — negotiable.

“Asli aur nakli mein farak kya hai?” Kshitij asked from the back seat.

Gabbar’s eyes found him in the rear-view mirror. For a second the showman went somewhere else.

“Nakli kahani main sunata hoon,” he said. “Asli kahani meri naani sunati thi.” He looked back at the road. “Woh main kisi ko nahi sunata.”

Then he grinned, honked at a cow, and turned to me. “Professor sahab. Savoy mein ruk rahe ho na? Wahan ek angrez memsahib mari thi. 1911 mein. Darwaza andar se band tha. Aaj tak koi nahi jaanta kaise.” He held up one finger. “Sau rupaye. Family discount.”

“That’s why I booked it,” said Shaoni from the back, without looking up from her phone. “It’s historic.”

“Didi,” said Kshitij, “historic aur haunted Hindi mein ek hi shabd hai.”

Kshitij speaks Hindi no better than I do, and yet nobody in that car could prove him wrong.

The Savoy sits off the Mall Road like an old aunty at a wedding: grand, Gothic, slightly disapproving of everyone who arrives after her. It opened in 1902. Viceroys danced here. Poets drank here. And, judging by the lobby, a man with a ring light was about to embarrass himself here.

“GHOST FAMILY, KYA HAAL HAI!” he shouted into a phone on a gimbal. “Main hoon Ghostbuster Golu, aur aaj hum LIVE hain India ke sabse haunted hotel se!”

A waiter carrying a tray of tomato soup walked straight through his shot. Golu screamed. The soup survived. Golu’s dignity did not.

I’m going to describe him carefully, because I want you to see him exactly the way I saw him that evening, and I want you to remember it later.

Late twenties. A shiny red puffer jacket that still had the fold-lines from its packet. A brand-new gimbal, a brand-new ring light, brand-new white sneakers that had never met mud. Everything about him was new and loud and bought last week.

Everything except his cap. It was a faded blue woollen cap, the wool gone thin and furry at the edges, with a small embroidered badge on the front so worn you couldn’t read it any more. It looked as if it had been to the top of something.

I noticed it. I filed it under YouTuber fashion. That’s the thing about noticing: it’s only half the job.

Ten minutes later, he ended his live stream the way, I would learn, he ended every single video. He leaned into the lens and winked.

“Aur yaad rakhna, ghost family: pahad pe akele mat jaana!”

The red light on his phone went off. He folded the gimbal. And I saw his lips move one more time, silently, saying the same five words to nobody.

My phone buzzed. Mummy.

I stepped away from the others and took it by the window. Behind me, Shaoni was explaining to the receptionist, slowly and in numbered points, why our booking said two rooms when she had clearly asked for two rooms and a reading lamp.

“Pahunch gaye?” asked my mother.

“Pahunch gaye, Mummy.”

“Sweater pehna?”

“Teen.”

“Good.” A pause. In Ahmedabad, I could hear the pressure cooker. “Aur Parth. Bhoot-vhoot ke peeche mat padna.”

Across the lobby, Golu was filming a curtain with great emotion.

“Mummy,” I said, “main bhoot ke peeche nahi padunga. Promise.”

That was the second lie of the trip. It was mine.

At dinner I opened my notebook. Brown leather, a little worn, a farewell gift from my final-year students with Sir, likhte rehna stamped inside the cover. I wanted to write down the Savoy case properly, because a real woman had died in this building and she deserved better than a ring light. Before I wrote a word, I closed my eyes and said a small prayer for her. Some habits come from your mother and some come from the mountains, and I have never been able to tell which is which.

Here are the facts, as best anyone knows them.

In 1911, Miss Frances Garnett-Orme, 49, a spiritualist, was staying at the Savoy with her companion, Miss Eva Mountstephen, who was known for séances and crystal-gazing. On the morning of 19 September, while Miss Mountstephen was away in Lucknow, Miss Garnett-Orme was found dead in her bed. Her door was locked from the inside. The post-mortem found prussic acid: cyanide. Miss Mountstephen was tried for murder and acquitted, and the judge said the truth would probably never be known. A few years later, people say, a young nurse named Agatha Christie wrote her first novel, about a poisoning behind a locked door.

I read it out to the table.

Kshitij was the first to speak. “Toh kisne kiya?”

“Court ne kaha, unhone nahi kiya,” Shaoni told him. She was cutting her roti into exact quarters. “Locked room. Poison. Companion out of town. Teen options: kisi ke paas doosri chaabi thi, ya unhone khud liya, ya police aane se pehle saboot saaf kar diya gaya.” She ate a quarter. “Teen options. Zero proof.”

“Bhoot ne kiya,” said Gabbar, who had pulled up a chair for dessert and had very firm views.

“Or,” said a voice behind me, “someone who understood that the best place to hide a secret is inside a story everyone already thinks they know.”

I turned round.

He was about forty, in a navy quarter-zip that probably cost more than my monthly salary, with the kind of face you’d trust to sell you a mutual fund. He smiled at me as if we were old friends who had lost touch.

“Sorry,” he said. “Occupational hazard. Rohan Sehgal. I run treks: Summit Secrets. You’ve probably seen our ads.”

Shaoni had. She’d almost booked one, until she saw the price.

Behind him, at the next table, sat a very large man in a black jacket, eating soup with great concentration and no visible joy. He didn’t introduce himself. Nobody introduced him. He was the kind of man who doesn’t need introducing, the way a wardrobe doesn’t.

Rohan pulled up a chair without being asked, which is a thing rich people can do, and for twenty minutes he was the best dinner company I’ve ever had. He knew the year every hotel in Mussoorie opened. He knew which Garhwali songs were about lovers and which were about landslides. He told Gabbar, in fluent Garhwali, a shepherd’s word for snow that has stopped falling but hasn’t yet settled, and Gabbar, who does not impress easily, raised his eyebrows.

Something happened during those twenty minutes that I didn’t notice and Kshitij did, so I’ll tell it as he told it to me later.

The Savoy’s dining room had an old speaker in one corner, playing soft instrumental music: a sitar, then a santoor, then, for one track, a bansuri playing an old Garhwali folk tune, the kind shepherds play on the high meadows. When the flute began, the large man’s spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. It stayed there, dripping, for the whole three minutes of the song. He didn’t look up. He didn’t move. When the track changed, he put the spoon down, pushed the bowl away, and didn’t touch it again.

Kshitij wrote one line in his diary: Big man doesn’t like flutes. Then he added a question mark.

“And Nanda Devi?” I asked Rohan, because I am a professor and cannot stop asking questions. “Aapne climb kiya hai?”

Something flickered behind his smile, just for a second, like a tubelight before it catches.

“Nobody climbs Nanda Devi any more, Professor,” he said lightly. “The sanctuary’s been closed for years. Officially for ecology.” He reached across and picked up my notebook. He didn’t ask. He turned it over in his hands, opened the cover, read Sir, likhte rehna, and smiled. “Nice. I have one just like it.”

He put it down again, and then he did a small, strange thing. He squared it to the edge of the table, exactly parallel, and straightened it one millimetre more.

Kshitij wrote another line and underlined it twice.

Rohan stood up. “Enjoy the Savoy, all of you. And don’t believe everything the ghosts tell you.”

The large man followed him out, leaving half his soup. In the corner by the fireplace, Golu was filming the flames, or at least his phone was pointed that way. Its little red light was facing our table. I didn’t think about it. I should have.

Gabbar watched the two men go, and for the first time since Dehradun he didn’t make a joke.

“Woh aadmi,” he said, to nobody in particular, “pahad ki baat aise karta hai jaise pahad uska ho.”

At 2:14 a.m., I was awake for three reasons. The bed was too soft. The room was too quiet. And somewhere in the corridor outside Room 112, someone was talking.

Not ghost-talking. Not the soft singing the legend promises. Man-talking: low, fast, annoyed.

I’d like to tell you I went to investigate out of scientific curiosity. I went because I’m nosy. I wrapped myself in the hotel’s spare shawl, white, enormous, very warm, picked up my notebook because I take it everywhere, and opened the door.

The corridor stretched away under dim yellow lamps. At the far end, by the old service stairs, stood two shapes. One slim. One built like a wardrobe.

Rohan Sehgal and the soup man.

I pressed myself against a pillar. I had only ever seen people do this in films, and it turns out it makes you look exactly like a man pressing himself against a pillar.

Rohan was speaking. “ND-65 ka core abhi bhi wahi hai, Bhalla.” So the wardrobe had a name. “Glacier pighal raha hai. Har saal thoda aur. Do saal mein koi trekker usse Instagram pe daal dega. Usse pehle humein pahunchna hai.”

“Aur geet?” Bhalla’s voice was surprisingly soft for a man that size. “Aakhri hissa toh mila hi nahi, sir.”

“Maletha wala buddha jaanta hai. Woh bata dega.” A pause. “Sab bata dete hain, Bhalla. Bas sahi tarah poochna padta hai.”

He was holding a notebook. Brown leather. A little worn.

Exactly like mine.

ND-65. Core. Glacier. I teach computers, not physics, but I have watched enough documentaries at one in the morning to feel my stomach drop through the carpet.

I took one careful step backwards.

Into a room-service trolley.

The trolley, it turned out, was carrying every spoon in Mussoorie. They hit the marble like a brass band falling down a staircase.

Both men turned.

What Rohan and Bhalla saw at that moment (and I know this, because I have since seen the footage) was a tall white figure, wrapped head to foot, standing absolutely still at the end of a haunted corridor at 2:14 in the morning.

Bhalla took a step back. His hand went to his chest.

Rohan didn’t move at all. “Bhalla,” he said, very calmly. “Bhoot nahi hai. Pakdo.”

I ran.

I ran the way a man runs when he has spent twelve years telling students that physical fitness is important. Halfway down the corridor, I tripped on the shawl, went down hard, and my notebook skidded out of my hand across the carpet. Bhalla was right behind me. He hit the same spoons, slid, crashed shoulder-first into the wall, and the notebook in his hand, which Rohan had shoved at him to free his own hands, went sliding too.

Two brown leather notebooks. One dim corridor. One terrified professor.

I grabbed the nearest one and kept running.

At the corner, a blinding white light exploded in my face.

“Ghost family,” whispered a voice behind it, trembling with joy, “woh dekho… safed saaya…”

Golu. Ring light at full power. And it wasn’t pointed at me. It was pointed straight past me, directly into Bhalla’s eyes.

Later, I would wonder how Golu came to be standing at that corner, fully dressed, camera already rolling, before a single spoon had fallen. At the time I was busy staying alive.

Bhalla, blinded, ran into a door frame. Golu screamed. Bhalla screamed. I did not stop to find out who screamed louder. I reached Room 112, got inside, locked the door, and slid down it to the floor.

A locked door. The Savoy. The middle of the night.

I started laughing, and then I very much stopped.

“Tumne sapna dekha,” Shaoni told me at breakfast. She was buttering toast with surgical precision. “Heavy dinner. Unfamiliar hotel. Classic.”

“Main professor hoon,” I told her. “Main sapne mein spoons nahi giraata.”

Kshitij said nothing. He just turned his phone round and slid it across the table to his sister.

Golu’s video already had 1.2 million views. The title was REAL GHOST CAUGHT AT SAVOY (NOT CLICKBAIT!!!), and the thumbnail was a tall white blur. Me. In the description, under Spirits identified, I was listed as Unknown Entity (Female?).

Shaoni watched it twice. She put the toast down.

“Bhai,” Kshitij said to me, scrolling the comments, “aapka comment section aapse zyada famous hai.”

I watched it a third time. The ghost (me) was on screen for eleven seconds. The man at the far end of the corridor, half-turned, his face lit white by the ring light, was on screen for nineteen. For a ghost video, it was strangely interested in the living.

I put it down to bad editing.

Across the dining room, Golu was eating aloo paratha alone, and he wasn’t looking at his phone. He was watching Rohan Sehgal’s table, the way you watch a door you expect to open.

Rohan’s table was empty. Laid fresh. Untouched.

I asked the waiter.

“Mr Sehgal?” he said. “Checked out, sir. Five a.m.”

We went upstairs for our bags.

Room 112 had been searched.

Not trashed. Searched, carefully. Drawers opened and closed again, a few millimetres out of line. My suitcase unzipped and zipped back up. The mattress sitting very slightly crooked on its base, the way it sits when someone has lifted it and put it down in a hurry. Whoever had done it wanted me to know, and wanted me to know they’d been polite about it.

Shaoni stood in the doorway for a long moment. She wasn’t saying sapna any more.

“Parth,” she said slowly. “Chor hota toh wallet le jaata. Yeh kuch specific dhoondh raha tha.” She looked at the room number on the door. “Aur usko pata tha tum kaunse kamre mein ho.”

Logic, cold and fast, like a bucket of Mussoorie tap water.

Then she did something I don’t think she knew she was doing. She stepped sideways, very casually, until she was standing between Kshitij and the corridor. Kshitij noticed. He always does. He didn’t say anything; he just stood a little straighter, the way younger brothers do when they’re being protected and would prefer not to be.

I checked my bag. Wallet. Phone. Charger. Three sweaters.

No notebook. Not mine.

The only notebook in the room was the one I’d grabbed off the corridor floor at 2:14 and shoved under my pillow. Brown leather. A little worn.

I opened it.

There was no Sir, likhte rehna inside the cover.

Tucked into the first page was a black-and-white photograph, soft at the corners: a glacier, a ridgeline, a dark smudge on the snow. On the back, in fading blue ink: Spring 1966. Above the smudge, faint as breath, a curl of something rising from the ice.

Steam.

The next pages were coordinates, crossed out and rewritten. Expedition dates. Altitudes. A list of village names (Maletha, Dronagiri, Munsiyari), some ticked, some circled twice.

And then, in careful handwriting, a song.

Jahan paani ke badle beta diya,

wahan se pehla paath liya.

Jahan pathar ne nadi roki,

wahan Nanda ki aankh khuli…

Nanda ki garam shila, barf mein soyi,

jisne chhui, woh laut ke na royi…

The last verse had been torn out. You could see the ragged edge.

I did what any tech-savvy professor does when handed a dangerous secret: I photographed every page, uploaded the lot to an encrypted folder, and named it Unit 4 Practical Marks, because nobody in history has ever voluntarily opened that.

“Parth.” Shaoni’s voice had changed. “Gabbar bhaiya.”

Gabbar was standing in the doorway with the car keys. He’d come up to hurry us along. He wasn’t hurrying anyone now. He was looking at the open page over my shoulder, and all the colour had gone out of his face.

“Yeh geet,” he said quietly. He wasn’t saying it to me. He was saying it to the song. “Yeh meri naani gaati thi.”

Nobody said anything.

Then, because I am a professor and I always check the last page first, and I had somehow forgotten to, I turned to the last page of the notebook.

The ink was fresh. Still a little shiny. Written that morning, in the same careful hand as the song:

Professor Parth. Room 112.

Aap mera notebook rakhiye. Main aapka rakh leta hoon.

Phir milenge. — R.

Kshitij read it over my shoulder. Shaoni read it over his. Gabbar went to the window and looked down into the Savoy’s car park.

“Professor sahab,” he said, without turning round.

Down below, a black SUV with tinted glass had started its engine. It didn’t move. It just sat there, running, facing the gate.

It did not leave until we did.

Rule One: Never Look Back at the Ghat

Day 2·Devprayag road

The black SUV stayed four cars behind us all the way down the hill to Dehradun.

Not close. Not far. Just there, like a doubt you can’t shake in the middle of a lecture.

Shaoni leaned forward between the two front seats with Rohan’s notebook open on her knees and the look she gets when a problem has finally become interesting.

“Okay,” she said. “Facts.” She held up one finger at me, because I was in the front passenger seat and therefore the nearest audience. “Ek. Rohan Sehgal ke paas tumhara notebook hai. Do. Hamare paas uska. Teen. Uske notebook mein coordinates hain, glacier ki photo hai, aur ek shabd hai: core. Chaar.” She jerked her thumb at the rear window without looking. “Ek gaadi hamara peecha kar rahi hai.”

“Paanch,” said Kshitij from the back seat, without lifting his eyes from his diary. “Gabbar bhaiya ne chaalis minute se ek bhi joke nahi maara.”

Gabbar’s eyes flicked to the rear-view mirror and met Kshitij’s.

“Kyunki yeh mazaak nahi hai,” he said.

“Police?” I suggested.

Shaoni turned to me with exaggerated patience. “Aur kya bolenge, Parth? ‘Sir, ek aadmi ne hamare professor ka notebook le liya, aur hamare paas uska notebook hai’?” She snapped the notebook shut. “Technically, abhi chor hum hain.”

I’d like to tell you I had a counter-argument. Instead, I did what any tech-savvy professor does in a crisis. I Googled it.

ND-65. The results were a motorcycle part, a Korean skincare serum and a government tender for road repairs in Nagaland.

Nanda Devi 1965. The results were everything in the Introduction to this book. (If you skipped it, go back. I’ll wait. I’m very good at waiting for people who didn’t do the reading.)

I read it all in a moving car on a mountain road. By the time we passed Rishikesh, my stomach had stopped being a stomach and become a small, cold stone.

“Plutonium,” I said, out loud, to the windscreen.

Nobody in the car said anything for an entire hairpin bend.

Then Kshitij, very quietly, from the back: “Bhai. Yeh trip ke budget mein nahi tha.”

“Technically,” I told him, “plutonium ka half-life itna lamba hai ki yeh trip ka budget kabhi khatam hi nahi hoga. EMI pe chalega. Hamare pote bharenge.”

I laughed at my own joke. Nobody else did.

Shaoni leaned forward again, and this time she didn’t look at the notebook. She looked at me, with an expression I’ve seen only a handful of times in all the years I’ve known her.

“Parth,” she said, low enough that only I could hear. “Darr lag raha hai?”

“Mujhe? Main professor hoon. Main Deadpool hoon. Main—”

“Jitna bada joke, utna bada darr.” She said it the way you’d read out a formula. “Main tumhe jaanti hoon.” She sat back. “Theek hai. Mujhe bhi lag raha hai. Ab dono ko pata hai. Aage badho.”

That’s Shaoni. She will never hold your hand. She’ll just tell you, very precisely, that she knows it’s shaking, and then hand you a numbered list.

By the time we turned up the Alaknanda valley towards Devprayag, the sun had gone behind the ridges, and the road had become what mountain roads become at night: a strip of headlight with nothing on either side but the sound of the river.

The SUV was still behind us. It was no longer four cars back. There were no other cars.

Gabbar gripped the wheel with both hands, glanced at me, and cleared his throat like a man about to take a class.

“Professor sahab,” he said. “Aap padhate ho. Aaj main padhaunga.” He lifted one finger off the wheel without letting go of it, a skill they do not teach in Ahmedabad. “Pahad ke raat ke niyam. Dhyaan se suno, teeno.”

“Ek. Raat ko seeti mat bajao. Jo sunta hai, woh aa jaata hai.”

A second finger. “Do. Nadi kinare agar koi naam se bulaye, peeche mat dekho. Jawab mat do.”

A third. “Teen. Shamshan ghat ke paas se guzro toh sar jhuka ke guzro.”

“Kyun?” Kshitij asked from the back, pen ready.

Gabbar looked at him in the mirror for a long moment. Then he told us about the ghosts of the hills.

Not the polite English kind who stay in hotel corridors, he said. The pahadi kind. In Garhwal and Kumaon, people believe that those who die before their time (young, suddenly, far from home), and who never receive their proper last rites, don’t leave. They stay.

The most feared of them, he said, is Masan: the spirit of the cremation ground. Masan rules the river from one confluence to the next and lives where the pyres burn. It catches the young, girls and children mostly, and the people it catches fall sick in ways no doctor can treat. When a bride’s procession passes such a place, the elders circle rice and coins over her head and throw them behind her, so that whatever is following takes the offering and leaves the bride alone.

Then there is Chhal, which is not a ghost exactly but a trick. It waits near springs, rivers, crossroads and lonely paths, at noon and at dusk, and when it catches you, you get a sudden fright, a fever, a confusion you can’t explain. Chhal lag gaya, people say.

“Aur jungle mein?” Shaoni asked. It was the first question she’d asked about ghosts in her life. I noticed. So did Gabbar.

“Jungle mein Khabees,” he told her. “Bada. Purana. Jaanwar ki shakal bhi le sakta hai, aadmi ki bhi. Aur charwahe ki awaaz mein bulata hai. Musafir sochta hai koi dost paas mein hai, aur awaaz ke peeche raste se utar jaata hai.”

“Aur jo peeche jaata hai?” Kshitij asked.

Gabbar didn’t answer. He didn’t have to.

I’m a believer, reader. I’ve told you that. But I’m also a man who has written three research papers, and the believer and the researcher in my head were now holding hands very tightly and refusing to let go.

At 9:40 p.m., the SUV switched on its high beams and closed the gap.

The road ahead narrowed. On the left: rock. On the right: a long drop to the river, silver in the dark.

“Pakad ke baitho,” Gabbar told all of us. Then he switched off Basanti’s headlights.

Reader, I would like to describe the next ninety seconds calmly. I can’t. I had never been in a car with its lights off on a Himalayan road at night, and I do not recommend it, and if Gabbar Singh Negi ever offers to do it for you, say no. He drove from memory. He drove by the shape of the sky above the ridge. At one point I’m fairly sure he drove by smell. In the back seat, Shaoni had one hand clamped on the grab handle and the other clamped on her brother’s knee, and Kshitij, I swear, was still writing.

Then Gabbar swung hard left down a gravel track I hadn’t even seen, the river roared up at us out of the dark, and we stopped.

Devprayag. Below us, under a few weak lamps, two rivers met: the Bhagirathi, fast and green-grey, and the Alaknanda, wider and louder. From here, together, they become the Ganga.

And beside the water, on the stone steps of the ghat, the last orange glow of a pyre.

“Yahan kyun?” Shaoni whispered to Gabbar.

“Kyunki yahan raat ko koi peecha nahi karta, didi,” he whispered back. “Ghat pe nahi.”

Above us on the road, the SUV stopped. A door opened. Heavy footsteps crunched on gravel. A torch beam swept down the track and found Basanti.

Bhalla.

He came down the slope slowly, one big hand shading the torch. Ten metres. Eight. In the back seat, Shaoni reached for her door handle; I swear she was going to get out and argue with him. Gabbar caught her wrist between the seats without even turning his head.

Then he rolled his window down and spoke, quietly, into the dark.

“Bhai sahab. Yeh Masan ka ilaaka hai. Do sangamon ke beech.” He let that sit. “Raat ko yahan peeche se koi bulaye, toh mud ke mat dekhna.”

Bhalla stopped.

It was a very small stop, a hesitation of maybe half a second. But Kshitij saw it. And Kshitij, who had written flinched like he’d seen a real ghost in his diary the morning before, did the single most brilliant thing anyone did on this entire trip.

He held his phone out of the back window, turned the volume all the way up, and played Golu’s Savoy video.

Across the dark ghat, over the sound of two rivers, came Golu’s whisper (“Ghost family… woh dekho… safed saaya…”), followed by the scream. The real one. The soup scream.

Bhalla turned round.

He didn’t mean to. You could see him not meaning to. His body just did it, the way your hand jerks back from a hot pan. He turned to look behind him, at the pyre, at the river, at the dark: exactly what Gabbar had told him not to do.

And in the glow of the pyre, I saw his lips move. Not a curse. Something short, repeated, the way you’d mutter a prayer. Or an apology.

Gabbar hit the lights, slammed Basanti into reverse, spun her on gravel I wouldn’t have parked a bicycle on, and roared back up the track past the SUV before Bhalla had finished turning round again.

As we passed the confluence, I folded my hands. Partly for the rivers. Partly for whoever had been on that pyre. And partly, I admit, because I wanted somebody up there on our side.

Behind us, a tall figure stood on the steps of the ghat with the torch hanging from his hand, staring at the water.

Two kilometres later, Gabbar spoke again. He spoke to me, but loudly enough for the back seat.

“Professor sahab. Aap logon ne kabhi socha hai… itna bada aadmi bhoot se kyun darta hai?”

It was Kshitij who answered, quietly, already writing.

“Kyunki usne kuch dekha hai.”

We stopped at a dhaba past Srinagar a little before midnight: strong chai, rajma that had been on the stove since morning, and a fire burning in an oil drum. For the first time since Mussoorie, my hands stopped shaking enough to hold a glass.

Gabbar held out his hand to me across the table. “Notebook.”

I gave it to him. He turned to the page with the song and ran one thick finger under the lines, his lips moving. Then he did something none of us expected.

He sang it.

Softly, barely above the crackle of the fire, in a voice that sounded nothing like the man who had just driven blind down a mountain.

Jahan paani ke badle beta diya, wahan se pehla paath liya…

He stopped after four lines and looked into the fire for a while.

“Meri naani gaati thi,” he told us. “Kumaon ke upar wale gaon mein. Bachpan mein suna. Hum sochte the bas ek gaana hai. Ek garam pathar ki kahani.” He frowned, reaching for it. “Kehti thi, jis saal pahad pe helicopter aaye the… agle basant mein charwahon ne barf mein ek pathar dekha jisse bhaap uth rahi thi.”

Shaoni leaned in. “Uske baad?”

Gabbar shook his head. “Naani ne kabhi aage nahi sunaya, didi. Bas itna kehti thi: jisne chhua, woh laut ke na roya.”

“Matlab?” Kshitij asked him.

“Matlab jisne usse chhua, usne phir kabhi kisi baat pe nahi roya.” Gabbar paused. “Ya phir… woh laut ke hi nahi aaya.”

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, a dog barked twice and stopped.

Kshitij was the one who broke the silence. “Aapki naani ab—?”

“Bahut saal pehle.” Gabbar turned his chai glass round and round between his palms. “Ija hai abhi. Meri maa. Gaon mein. Akeli.” He was quiet for a moment. “Do saal ho gaye milne nahi gaya. Har saal sochta hoon, is Diwali jaunga. Har saal season aa jaata hai. Tourist aa jaate hain. Basanti ki EMI aa jaati hai.”

He shrugged, a big man’s shrug that didn’t quite work.

“Ija phone pe kehti hai, koi baat nahi, tu kaam kar. Pahadi maayein aisi hi hoti hain. Kabhi shikayat nahi karti. Isliye aur bura lagta hai.” He looked into the fire. “Is baar toh bulaya bhi tha. Gaon mein jagar hone wala hai, agle hafte. Ek ladke ke liye. Bujurg kehte hain usse Masan ne pakda hai. Ija boli, aa jaata toh achha lagta.”

“Aur aapne kya kaha?” Shaoni asked him gently.

“Maine kaha, season hai.”

He put the notebook down on the table very carefully, as if it belonged to her.

“Woh yeh geet gaati thi, aur main sunta nahi tha,” he said. “Mujhe sirf gaadiyon ka shauk tha. Socha tha, gaane toh hamesha rahenge.”

Nobody had a joke for that. Not even me.

That’s when a white SUV with a ring light on the dashboard pulled into the dhaba, and a man in a red puffer jacket got out, already filming.

“GHOST FAMILY!” Golu spotted us and pointed the camera straight at our table. “Kya coincidence hai! Wahi Savoy wali team! Main Kedarnath ja raha hoon, bhoot content ke liye. Aap log bhi?”

Shaoni’s eyes narrowed. She didn’t look at him. She looked at me. “Coincidence?”

“Bilkul, didi!” Golu answered brightly, though she hadn’t asked him. “Pahad mein ek hi road hai.”

Which, to be fair, is true.

He sat down two tables away, pulled the faded blue cap down over his ears against the cold, ordered Maggi, and went live. I could hear his phone pinging with comments: fifty, a hundred. He read them out in his loud, happy YouTube voice.

Then his phone pinged once more, and Golu stopped reading.

Just for a second. Then he laughed, too loudly, and changed the subject.

Kshitij was sitting closest to him. He got up as if to fetch more chai, leaned casually over Golu’s shoulder on the way past, and came back to our table with his face very still. He put his diary down in front of me, open, and pointed to the line he had just copied, word for word, from the comments under Golu’s live stream.

@ND65: Notebook wapas karo, Professor. Politely. Abhi ke liye.

I looked across at Golu. Golu was looking into the fire.

He had not, I realised, asked us a single question about why a car had just chased us down a mountain in the dark.

As if he already knew.

Chapter Three

The Water That Cost a Son

Day 3·Maletha and Dhari Devi

At breakfast, Gabbar put his finger on the first line of the song and tapped it twice, the way a teacher taps a word on the board.

“Jahan paani ke badle beta diya,” he read out. Then he looked round the table at the three of us as if we’d failed a very easy test. “Yeh toh har Garhwali bachcha jaanta hai. Maletha.”

Maletha was twelve kilometres back the way we’d come. We had driven past it in the dark, with a black SUV on our tail, and none of us had noticed a thing.

That’s the thing about Uttarakhand. You drive past a legend every five minutes. Most of them don’t have a signboard.

Gabbar told it on the way back, the way his grandmother had told it to him, one hand on the wheel and the other conducting the story.

Four hundred years ago, he said, Maletha was a dry village on a high, flat shelf above the Alaknanda. The fields were good, but the river was far below and the rains were never enough. Maletha’s most famous son was Madho Singh Bhandari, a general of the Garhwal kings: a man so brave, the old saying goes, that there were only three singhs worth counting in the world: the singh of a lion, the singh of a cow’s horn, and Madho Singh.

Coming home from war, Madho Singh found his own family hungry. So he decided to bring water to Maletha. Not with a pipe or a pump, because this was the 1600s. With his own hands, and his villagers’ hands, he cut a tunnel through the hillside from a stream on the far side, so the water could flow through the mountain and out onto Maletha’s fields.

The tunnel was finished. The water would not flow.

They prayed. They made offerings. Nothing. And the story says that a voice (a priest, a dream, a goddess, it depends who’s telling it) told Madho Singh what the mountain wanted in return.

His son.

Kshitij’s pen stopped. “Aur unhone diya?”

“Kahani kehti hai, haan,” Gabbar told him, quietly, to the mirror. “Gajendra naam tha. Unhone apne bete ki bali di. Aur paani beh gaya.”

Nobody in the car said anything for a long time.

It was Shaoni who finally asked. Not challenging. Just asking. “Is that true?”

She was asking me, because I’m the one who reads things at one in the morning.

“The canal is true,” I told her. “It’s still running. Madho Singh was real. The sacrifice is legend. Nobody can prove it either way.”

“Toh phir?”

Gabbar answered before I could. “Toh phir, didi, Maletha ke log aaj bhi usse yaad karte hain. Aur paani aaj bhi aata hai.”

That’s the other thing about Uttarakhand. True and legend don’t fight here. They share a bench.

The canal at Maletha is not dramatic, and that is what makes it hard to look at. It’s a narrow channel of clear water running along the edge of green terraced fields, through a village where children walk to school and a man was fixing a motorbike outside a kirana shop. It’s ordinary. It is the most ordinary miracle I have ever seen.

We found the old man at the third house past the temple.

He was sitting on his stone porch in a grey shawl, small and thin and very upright, with a hookah he wasn’t smoking and eyes that had clearly seen us coming up the path. The neighbours told us his name was Bachi Ram ji. Ninety-one. The last of a shepherd family that used to take its flocks up to the high meadows every summer, all the way up towards Nanda Devi.

Gabbar greeted him in Garhwali, touching his feet. The old man blessed him absently. His eyes had already gone to the notebook under my arm, and he went very still.

“Woh aaya tha,” he said, switching to Hindi so all of us would understand. “Do din pehle. Sehgal. Bahut tameez se baat karta hai. Mithai laaya tha.” He looked away at the hills. “Aur ek bada aadmi saath mein. Jo kuch nahi bolta.”

Bhalla.

“Usne kya poocha, dada ji?” Shaoni asked him.

“Geet. Mere dada wala geet.”

“Aapne bataya?”

Bachi Ram ji’s hands tightened on his shawl. “Usne mere pote ka naam liya. Dehradun mein padhta hai. Hostel ka naam bhi pata tha usko.” He swallowed. “Haan. Bataya.”

I’ve taught for twelve years. I’ve seen every kind of fear in a young face: before results, before parents, before the future. I had never seen it in a face that was ninety-one years old. It made something hot and furious climb up the back of my neck.

Shaoni crouched down in front of him, until her eyes were level with his.

“Dada ji,” she said. “Woh aadmi jo dhoondh raha hai, woh bahut khatarnaak cheez hai. Agar use mil gayi, toh sirf aapke gaon ka nahi, poori Ganga ke paani ka nuksaan hoga. Hum usse rokne ki koshish kar rahe hain. Aapko darna nahi padega.” She paused. “Bas ek baar woh geet humein bhi suna dijiye.”

I have never once in my life seen Shaoni ask anybody for anything. She tells. She instructs. She argues with traffic police and wins. But she asked him. And she said it in a voice I’d never heard from her (completely calm, completely fearless), as if the most dangerous man in the state hadn’t been sitting on that same porch two days earlier.

Bachi Ram ji looked at her for a long time.

Then he sang.

His voice was cracked and thin and absolutely sure of itself, like an old path that has been walked so many times it can’t be lost. He sang the four lines we already had. Then two more, about a steaming stone in the snow.

Then he stopped, took a breath, and began again from the beginning, as old singers do.

Kshitij leaned towards me without taking his eyes off the old man, and whispered, “Bhai. Phir se. Dhyaan se suno.”

The third time, I heard it too.

The line in Rohan’s notebook said: Jahan pathar ne nadi roki. Where the stone stopped the river.

The first time, Bachi Ram ji had sung pathar ne nadi roki. The second time, he sang pathar ne Mandakini roki. And the third time, pathar ne Kedar ki nadi roki.

One word, changed every time, the way a singer drops in the local place-name when he knows exactly where the story happened.

The Mandakini is the river that comes down from Kedarnath.

Shaoni saw it at the same moment. “The stone that stopped the river,” she said slowly, to Kshitij. “In 2013, during the flood. The rock behind Kedarnath temple. The Bhim Shila.”

“Which didn’t exist in 1966,” I said to both of them. “Not as a famous rock. It only came down in 2013.”

“Toh geet 1966 ka nahi hai.” Kshitij was already writing the three versions one under the other and circling the changed word. “Ya kam se kam, yeh line 1966 ki nahi hai. Kisi ne geet ko update kiya hai. 2013 ke baad.”

Reader, I am a professor. I have a laptop, three degrees of anxiety and the internet. Kshitij has a diary and a pen. Main professor hoon. Notes yeh banata hai.

I turned back to the old man. “Dada ji. Yeh line… Kedar ki nadi… aap kab se aise gaate hain?”

For the first time, Bachi Ram ji smiled.

“Bete, geet zinda hote hain,” he told me. “Jo dekhte hain, woh jodte rehte hain. Mere dada ne pathar dekha tha. Maine baad mein Kedar ka pathar dekha. Toh maine joda.” He shrugged. “Pahad ne pathar rakha. Bhagwan ne mandir bachaya. Dono baat ek hi geet mein aa jaati hai.”

Then he leaned forward, lowered his voice, and gave us something that wasn’t in Rohan’s notebook at all.

“Aakhri hissa main nahi jaanta. Mere dada kehte the, woh hissa Dronagiri wale charwahe jaante hain. Woh pathar unhone dekha tha. Pehle.”

On our way out of the village, I saw something that at the time I filed under funny and forgot.

Golu was there.

Of course he was. He was standing at the edge of the canal, filming the water for what I assumed was a video called HAUNTED CANAL OF BLOOD (REAL). Except he wasn’t filming. He was talking to a woman drawing water, and he was talking to her in Garhwali. Not tourist Garhwali. Not namaste-dajyu Garhwali. Real, fast, laughing Garhwali, the way you talk to somebody’s aunty when you’ve known aunties like her your whole life.

The woman laughed at something he said, reached up the way pahadi aunties do, and straightened the old blue cap on his head as if he were a schoolboy. And for one second, Golu let her. He stood perfectly still and let her, with his eyes closed.

Then he saw us. His YouTube voice switched on like a light.

“GHOST FAMILY! Maletha ki khooni nahar! Comment karo agar aapko bhi lagta hai yahan aatma hai!”

Kshitij wrote something down. I didn’t ask him what.

Dhari Devi was the next stop on the road to Rudraprayag, and it was the one place on this trip where none of us said anything funny at all.

The temple sits on the Alaknanda, raised now on a platform above the river. For centuries the goddess here was worshipped on a rock at the water’s edge. In June 2013, her idol was moved from its old place to make way for a hydropower project. On that very evening, people say, the sky opened over Kedarnath, and the flood came down the valleys and killed thousands.

The engineers will tell you about cloudbursts and moraine lakes and rainfall data. The people of the valley will tell you the goddess was moved and the mountain answered. I believe the engineers. I also believe the people of the valley. I’ve stopped thinking those two things can’t stand on the same riverbank.

I climbed the steps alone and stood in front of her and folded my hands. I didn’t ask for anything. I just stood there for a while, a very small professor from the plains with a stolen notebook in his bag and a plutonium problem, and let the sound of the river fill my head.

I didn’t hear anyone sit down behind me.

“Beautiful, isn’t it?”

A man in a navy jacket was sitting two steps below me, looking at the river, not at me. Clean shoes. Expensive watch. On the left side of his chest, a small embroidered logo: a mountain inside a circle, and the words SUMMIT SECRETS.

He didn’t turn round.

“Mr Sehgal would like to meet you, Professor,” he said pleasantly, to the river. “He’s a fan. He read your notebook last night. All of it.” A pause. “Especially the parts about your mother.”

Then he stood, brushed off his trousers, walked down the steps and away across the bridge, unhurried, just another pilgrim.

I stayed where I was, hands still folded, and for the first time on this trip, I prayed for something specific.

Then I called her.

“Mummy? Nahi, bas aise hi. Haan, khana kha liya. Haan, teen. Nahi, koi bhoot nahi. Mausam? Bahut achha hai. Haan. Haan. Theek hai. Rakhta hoon.”

Forty seconds. I made two jokes in forty seconds, and she laughed at both, and when I hung up, my hands were shaking so badly I couldn’t get the phone back into my pocket.

Shaoni sat down on the step beside me. She’d seen the man leave. She didn’t ask what he’d said. She took the phone out of my hand, put it in my pocket for me, and spoke in her flattest, most boring spreadsheet voice.

“Ek. Aunty Ahmedabad mein hain. Rohan pahad mein hai. Do. Usne dhamki di hai, kuch kiya nahi hai. Teen. Dhamki tab dete hain jab kuch chahiye hota hai. Matlab hum abhi bhi useful hain.” She paused. “Chaar.”

“Chaar?”

“Chaar. Tum akele nahi ho.”

“Point four logic nahi hai, Shaoni.”

“Point four data hai,” said Shaoni. She stood up and held out her hand to pull me up, which she has never once done before or since.

Chapter Four

The Rock That Stopped a River

Day 4·Kedarnath

The moment the doors of Basanti shut, Kshitij spoke from the back seat.

“Rohan galat jagah ja raha hai.”

All three of us turned to look at him. Shaoni twisted sideways beside him; Gabbar and I used the mirrors. He had his diary open on his knees, the three versions of the verse written one under the other.

“Dekho.” He held the page up between the front seats so I could see it. “Rohan ke notebook mein likha hai pathar ne nadi roki. Usne Bachi Ram ji se geet suna, shayad Kedar ki nadi wala version. Toh woh sochega ki garam shila Kedarnath mein hai. Bhim Shila ke paas.” He tapped the page. “Par Bachi Ram ji ne khud kaha: Kedar wali line unhone baad mein jodi. 2013 ke baad. Woh line jagah nahi batati. Woh bas ek naya chamatkar jodti hai.”

Shaoni folded her arms. “Toh hum Kedarnath kyun ja rahe hain?”

She was asking the way she always asks: not to argue, but to make you prove it.

And Kshitij smiled. It was the first time I’d seen him smile since Mussoorie.

“Kyunki 1966 mein, jo charwahe Dronagiri se garam shila dekh ke aaye the, woh kahaan jaate?” He looked from his sister to Gabbar. “Itni badi baat ho, toh pahadi aadmi kya karta hai?”

Gabbar answered slowly, as if the words were arriving one at a time. “Bhagwan ke paas jaata hai.”

“Aur Kedarnath ke purohit,” said Kshitij, “har yatri ka naam likhte hain. Peedhi dar peedhi. Bahi-khaate mein.”

There was a silence in the car.

I’d like to tell you I’d thought of that. I’m the professor. As a boy in Haridwar, I’d watched a panda flip through a huge cloth-bound register and find my great-grandfather’s name, in his own handwriting, from 1931. I knew those registers existed. I just hadn’t connected it.

Kshitij had.

“Bhai,” I said, turning round to him properly, “main professor hoon. Tum notes banate ho. Mujhe lagta hai hum roles badal lete hain.”

“Nahi.” Kshitij was already writing again. “Aapko trekking karni hai.”

Beside him, Shaoni didn’t say anything. But she reached across and squeezed her brother’s shoulder once, quickly, the way you’d tap a lucky charm, and then pretended she’d been adjusting her seatbelt.

On the long drive up the Mandakini valley, Gabbar told Shaoni a story. I’m certain he told it for her on purpose, because he caught her eye in the mirror before he began.

“Didi. Aapko Tilu Rauteli pata hai?”

She shook her head.

In the seventeenth century, he told her, in a village in Chaundkot, in what is now Pauri Garhwal, there lived a girl named Tilottama. Tilu. In those years, raiders from across the hills attacked her region again and again. By the time she was fifteen, her father had been killed in the fighting. So had her two brothers. So had the young man she was going to marry.

That year, Tilu wanted to go to the fair at Kanda. Bright clothes, music, swings: all the things a fifteen-year-old should want.

Her mother, broken by grief, looked at her and said: Tilu, tujhe mela yaad hai? Apne bhai yaad nahi? Agar kahin jaana hai, toh ladaai ke maidan mein ja.

Tilu didn’t go to the fair.

She went to war.

The ballads say she gathered her father’s men, rode at their head, and fought seven wars between the ages of fifteen and twenty. People in Garhwal call her their Lakshmibai. And they say that one day, after all those battles, she stopped by a river to bathe, alone, and an enemy soldier who had been hiding there killed her while she was unarmed.

When he finished, Shaoni spoke very quietly. “Bas? Sirf bees saal?”

“Bees saal, didi,” Gabbar told her. “Aur teen sau saal se log gaate hain.”

Shaoni looked out of the window at the river for a long time. When she turned back, there was something in her face I’d only seen before when someone had told her she couldn’t do something.

“Bhalla bada hai,” she said, to nobody in particular. “Tilu ke dushman bhi bade the.”

I decided not to ask what that meant. I’m a professor, not a fool.

But I’ll tell you what I think it meant, now that I know her better. All her life, people have told Shaoni (politely, at counters, across tables) that some things are too big for her. And all her life, she has treated those sentences as a to-do list. Gabbar hadn’t told her a story. He’d handed her a mirror, and she liked what she saw in it.

In the rear-view mirror, Gabbar smiled under his moustache. Thirty years of driving tourists had taught him exactly which story to tell which passenger. This was the first time on the trip, I think, that he’d used that skill for something other than tips.

The walk from Gaurikund to Kedarnath is about sixteen kilometres, and it goes up the whole way. That’s not a complaint. That’s a fact that my knees would like included in this book.

I would also like to address the author of this chapter, who is me, and ask him why he couldn’t have set this scene in a café.

Around kilometre six, Kshitij fell into step beside me. Shaoni was up ahead, setting a pace like a woman late for a board meeting. Gabbar was somewhere behind us, negotiating with a mule owner on principle.

Kshitij walked beside me for a while without speaking. Then: “Bhai. Woh bahi wala idea. Aapko sach mein achha laga?”

“Kshitij. Woh is trip ka sabse achha idea hai. Mere idea se bhi achha. Aur mere idea mein Google tha.”

He walked on. His ears had gone red, though that might have been the altitude.

“Ghar pe,” he said eventually, “sab Didi se poochte hain. Bachpan se. Kaunsa school, kaunsi train, fridge kaunsa lena hai. Didi ke paas hamesha plan hota hai. Main bas…” He lifted the diary a little. “Main bas likh leta hoon. Baad mein jab sab bhool jaate hain ki kisne kya kaha tha, tab sab mujhse poochte hain. Tab main useful hota hoon. Baad mein.”

“Aur aaj?”

“Aaj pehli baar,” he said, watching his sister’s red jacket on the trail above us, “Didi ne gaadi mein mujhse pehle poocha. Mere bolne se pehle.” A pause. “Unhe pata nahi chala. Mujhe chala.”

I’ve had a lot of students, reader. I know what it looks like when someone has been waiting years to be asked. I didn’t say anything wise. I just said, “Next clue bhi tumhara,” and he said, “Obviously,” and we walked on.

Around kilometre nine, wheezing against a railing while a seventy-year-old grandmother from Maharashtra overtook me with a cheerful “Jai Bhole!”, I looked back down the trail. Below us, in the river of yellow raincoats and walking sticks, one shape was standing still while everyone else moved.

Big. Black jacket. No raincoat.

Bhalla.

Shaoni had come back down to us. She saw him at the same moment I did, and she didn’t turn her head even slightly.

“Mat dekho,” she told me, through her teeth. “Chalte raho.”

Kedarnath opens on you all at once. You climb and climb through cloud and mud, and then the valley widens and there it is: the old grey stone temple, small and stubborn against a wall of white mountains, as if it was here before the mountains and plans to be here after.

And behind it, a rock.

A single boulder as big as a small truck, resting on the slope directly behind the temple.

In June 2013, when the flood came down from above (water, mud, ice and stones the size of houses), this rock rolled down with it and stopped. Right there. Behind the temple. And the water split around it, left and right, like a river around a pillar. Buildings all around were swept away. The temple stood.

People here call it the Bhim Shila.

I am a man who explains things. I stood in front of that rock and tried. Slope gradient. Momentum. Debris flow. A boulder lodging against a terrace. Probability.

Then I stopped. “Probability” felt like too small a word for what I was looking at. A woman next to me was crying with her hands folded, and if I’m honest, so was I.

I’m a believer, reader. Believers don’t need the rock to be a miracle. We just need it to be there.

The purohit family Gabbar took us to had kept pilgrim registers for longer than anyone could remember. The current keeper, a cheerful, round-faced man in his fifties named Pandit Umesh ji, listened to our question, laughed, and sent a boy running for the right volume.

“1966? Basant ka mahina? Charwahe?” He licked his finger and turned the brittle pages with the speed of a man who knew them better than his own hand. “Hamare bahi mein sab hai, beta. Raja bhi. Charwahe bhi. Bhagwan ke saamne sab barabar.”

Kshitij leaned so far over the register that his nose nearly touched it.

And there it was. A page dated Jyeshth, 1966, in faded blue-black ink. A group of pilgrims from Dronagiri. Three names.

Pratap Singh Rawat. Bhawan Singh. Kundan Singh.

In the margin, in a different, smaller hand, were two words that made Pandit Umesh ji frown and push his glasses up his nose.

Garam pathar.

“Yeh mere dada ji ki likhawat hai,” he told us slowly. “Woh bahut kam extra cheezein likhte the. Agar likha, toh charwahon ne kuch bataya hoga. Kuch aisa jo unhe yaad rakhna zaroori laga.”

Kshitij copied all three names into his diary. Then he underlined one of them twice.

Rawat.

I didn’t know why yet. Neither did he, really. He just had a feeling, and Kshitij writes his feelings down.

The fog came down while we were walking back.

In the mountains, fog doesn’t arrive. It happens. One minute you can see the trail winding below you for a kilometre; the next, you can see your own hand, and maybe Shaoni’s back, and nothing else at all.

And somewhere behind us in the white: heavy footsteps.

Shaoni stopped and caught my sleeve.

“Parth,” she whispered. “Kshitij ke saath aage chalo. Neeche wale shelter tak. Mujhe do minute do.”

“Bilkul nahi—”

“Parth.” She looked at me, and it was Tilu’s look. “Logic. Woh mujhe nahi dhoondh raha. Notebook tumhare paas hai. Mujhe sirf usse do minute bhatkaana hai.”

Then she was gone into the fog.

Here is what Shaoni did, as she explained it to us twenty minutes later in the shelter, completely calm, drinking chai as if she’d just finished a spreadsheet.

She stepped off the main trail onto a side path that led to a small, flat meadow and nowhere else. She took off her bright red jacket and hung it on a signboard at the meadow’s edge, where a torch would catch it. Then she walked back onto the main trail on the far side of a big rock, and stood perfectly still.

Bhalla came up out of the fog. His torch swept the trail and found nothing. It swept the side path and caught a flash of red.

He went for the red.

And Shaoni walked quietly down the main trail behind him in the opposite direction, in a grey fleece, like a ghost.

“Usne wahan pandrah minute khade rehke ek jacket se baat ki,” she told us, and sipped her chai. “Main sun sakti thi.”

I looked at her. She looked back. Neither of us mentioned that she had just done to Bhalla exactly what the old stories warn you a forest spirit will do: lead a traveller off the path with a flash of something familiar.

Gabbar was grinning for the first time all day. “Tilu Rauteli,” he said to her.

“Logic,” said Shaoni.

We came down to the base after dark: wet, exhausted, and very pleased with ourselves. That was the mistake.

Shaoni and I went inside to argue with the guesthouse owner about hot water. Gabbar went to move Basanti. Kshitij sat down on the bench outside the door and told us he’d write up the day while it was fresh.

When I came back out four minutes later, the bench was empty.

His diary was lying open on it, face up, the pen still tucked into the spine. The page was half written. The last line stopped in the middle of a word.

I looked up and down the road. Nothing. No footsteps. No engine. Just the sound of the Mandakini, very loud in the dark.

Kshitij never leaves his diary.

Not to eat. Not to sleep. Not ever.

Chapter Five

Five Pieces of a God

Day 5·Chopta, Tungnath, Chandrashila

I won’t describe that night properly. I can’t, and you don’t need me to.

We searched the road with torches until midnight. Gabbar woke half the village, knocking on doors and shouting in Garhwali. Shaoni called the police, and the police said what police say about a twenty-something man missing for three hours in a pilgrimage town: subah tak dekhte hain, sahab, shayad kahin chai pee raha hoga. Shaoni told the man on the phone, in very clear Hindi, exactly what she thought of that. I had never heard her raise her voice before. I hope I never do again.

I sat on the bench where Kshitij’s diary had been, holding it in both hands, and did the only useful thing I could think of. I prayed. Not the polite kind. The kind where you make promises you aren’t sure you can keep.

At 5:52 a.m., my phone buzzed. Unknown number.

A photo. Kshitij, sitting on a plastic chair outside what looked like a roadside dhaba, wrapped in a blanket, holding a cup of chai. Unhurt. Annoyed. Alive.

Underneath:

Chopta. 10 baje. Dhaba ke bahar. Ladka theek hai. Hum jaanwar nahi hain, Professor. — R.

Shaoni read it over my shoulder and sat down on the bench very suddenly, as if someone had cut the strings in her legs.

Then she stood up again.

“Chalo,” she said to Gabbar, who already had the keys in his hand.

Chopta is a meadow on a ridge, ringed by forests of rhododendron and oak, with the whole Himalaya lined up across the horizon like a jury. In spring, people say, it’s the most beautiful place in Garhwal. In late October, at 9:50 in the morning, with my heart going like a garba drummer on the final night of Navratri, I didn’t notice a single tree.

Kshitij was sitting outside the dhaba exactly as in the photo. Blanket. Chai. Nobody else around except the owner, who clearly had no idea anything strange had happened and wanted to know whether we’d like aloo parathas.

Shaoni got to him first. I’ll leave that part out. It’s theirs.

When I reached them, Kshitij looked up at me and said, absolutely seriously:

“Bhai. Meri diary?”

I gave it to him. He held it in both hands for a second, the way you’d hold something returned from the dead. Then he bent down, pulled off his left shoe, peeled off his sock, and took out a folded, sweaty petrol-pump receipt covered on both sides in tiny handwriting.

“Unhone meri diary nahi li,” he told me, “toh maine nayi bana li.”

Reader, I have taught for twelve years. I have supervised hundreds of projects. I have never, ever been prouder of a student, and he isn’t even mine.

We sat him down at the dhaba’s one table, and Shaoni ordered him three parathas without asking, and Kshitij told us what had happened, reading from the receipt.

Two men had grabbed him from the bench. Not violently, he said; professionally, like movers handling a sofa they didn’t care about. They put him in the back of a dark SUV and drove for about two hours, up the valley and over a ridge. Bhalla sat in the front passenger seat. A second man drove. Nobody hurt him.

Then, at a dhaba in the dark, the driver put a phone on speaker, and Rohan’s voice came out of it.

“Woh polite tha,” Kshitij said. “Hamesha polite hota hai. Usne poocha Kedarnath mein humein kya mila.”

“Tumne kya bola?” Shaoni asked him.

“Maine kaha, darshan. Kuch nahi mila. Sirf darshan. Aur Bhim Shila dekh ke main bahut emotional ho gaya tha.”

I almost laughed. “Usne maana?”

“Nahi.” Kshitij shrugged at me. “Par usne mujhe chai pilaayi aur bola: ‘Professor ko bolo, Auli mein milte hain. Notebook ke saath. Akele. Sunday ko. Hum sab samajhdaar log hain.’”

Then they’d driven him here, left him at dawn with a blanket, and paid for the chai.

He spread the receipt on the table between us. On it, in handwriting so small I needed my glasses:

  • The SUV’s number plate, written backwards so it would look like a scribble if anyone found it.
  • Boots: same brand as Bhalla. Same grey silt.
  • Driver said “Joshimath pump pe diesel” on the phone. (Joshimath: the town below Auli.)
  • Bhalla doesn’t sleep. Keeps checking the back window. Scared of the dark?
  • Driver put on old pahadi songs. Bhalla switched it off. Twice. Second time he said “Gaana nahi.” Very quiet. Not angry. Like it hurt.
  • They talk too much.

And at the very bottom, circled:

  • Rating: 3 stars. Chai achhi thi. Bahut baatein karte hain.

Shaoni read the last line and laughed (the first real laugh in fourteen hours), then cried a little, then pretended she had done neither.

Later, when she had gone to argue with the dhaba owner about the price of the parathas, a sign she had fully recovered, I sat down next to Kshitij and asked the question nobody else had.

“Darr laga?”

He looked at his receipt for a long time.

“Haan,” he told me. “Bahut. Pehle ghante mein haath kaanp rahe the. Phir maine socha: agar main likh raha hoon, toh main sirf ek ladka nahi hoon jisko utha liya gaya. Main ek ladka hoon jo notes le raha hai.” He folded the receipt carefully along its creases. “Darr wahi hai, bhai. Bas usko ek kaam de diya.”

I’ve read a lot of books about courage, reader. That is the best definition I’ve come across, and it was written on the back of a petrol-pump receipt by a boy in one sock.

At eleven, a white SUV with a ring light on the dashboard bumped up the Chopta road and stopped beside us in a spray of red mud.

Golu jumped out, filthy to the knees, already talking. “GHOST FAMILY! Raat bhar Tungnath ke bhoot ko dhoondha! Kuch nahi mila, par mera GPS bhi kho gaya tha—”

Shaoni turned round on the bench and looked at him.

I’ve told you about Shaoni’s looks. This was a new one. It took his mud-splattered car, his tired, red-rimmed eyes and his story, and quietly put them on a scale.

We had two days until Sunday. Shaoni wanted to go straight to Joshimath police. Gabbar wanted to go home and pretend none of this was happening. I wanted to sit down somewhere high and think.

It was Kshitij who settled it, looking up at the ridge above the meadow.

“Tungnath,” he said. “Upar. Jahan koi nahi aayega. Hum sochenge.”

So in the afternoon, we climbed.

The path from Chopta to Tungnath is steep and stone-paved, and it does not care about your feelings. Tungnath temple sits at about 3,680 metres. “Duniya ka sabse ooncha Shiv mandir,” Gabbar announced proudly to all three of us, as if he’d built it himself. It’s a small, dark, ancient shrine with snow peaks on three sides and the sky so close you feel you could touch it with a walking stick.

On the temple steps, Gabbar told us why it was here. This time I just listened, because this is one of those stories you should hear in the place it belongs to.

After the great war of the Mahabharata, he said, the Pandavas came to the Himalaya to be cleansed of the sin of killing their own kin. They wanted Shiva’s forgiveness. Shiva did not want to give it. So he hid from them: he took the form of a bull and mixed into a herd grazing in these hills.

Bhima found him. Bhima, of course, the strong one. He stood astride a valley, one foot on each hillside, and let the herd pass beneath him. Every bull walked through. One bull refused.

When Bhima grabbed it, the bull began to sink into the earth. Bhima held on to its back, and the god vanished, but his body appeared in five places across the Garhwal Himalaya.

“The hump at Kedarnath,” Gabbar counted off on his fingers. “The arms here, at Tungnath. The face at Rudranath. The navel at Madhyamaheshwar. The matted hair at Kalpeshwar.”

The Panch Kedar. Five pieces of a god, scattered over the mountains, and pilgrims who walk to all five.

“Toh har jagah thoda sa bhagwan hai,” said Kshitij, writing.

“Pahad mein har jagah thoda sa bhagwan hai, beta,” Gabbar told him. “Bas Panch Kedar mein address likha hai.”

I wrote that one down myself. Some lines you steal from your driver.

Far below, in a village on the side of the valley, drums started up. Not a temple drum: a dhol and damau, rehearsing.

Gabbar tilted his head to listen. “Pandav Lila,” he told us. “Sardiyon mein shuru hoti hai. Gaon wale Mahabharata khelte hain. Raat raat bhar.” He paused, choosing his words with care. “Aur kabhi kabhi, jo Bhim banta hai, uspe sach mein Bhim aa jaate hain.”

I listened to the drums coming up through the cold air, and I thought about how many ways there are in these mountains for something old to come back into the present. A song updated after a flood. A name in a priest’s register. A god in five pieces. A hero who returns into a villager’s body every winter.

And a warm stone, in the snow.

We went up the last stretch to Chandrashila for sunset: the summit above Tungnath, where Nanda Devi, Trishul, Chaukhamba and the Kedar peaks all line up in gold. We weren’t the only ones. An old man was sitting on a rock at the top, wrapped in an army-surplus jacket so faded it had no colour left, drinking tea from a flask.

He held the flask out to us without a word. Pahad mein, someone always offers you tea.

His name was Dorje. He’d been a cook and a load-carrier for expeditions in these mountains since he was a boy, he told us. When Gabbar mentioned where we were headed next, Dorje looked at the far peak glowing on the horizon (Nanda Devi, the highest, the most beautiful) and spoke almost to himself.

“Main gaya tha wahan. Chhiyasath mein. Bees saal ka tha.”

Nineteen sixty-six.

I didn’t breathe.

“Ek team gayi thi,” he went on, to the mountain rather than to us. “Angrez aur Hindustani. Kuch dhoondhne. Humein nahi bataya kya. Bas itna kaha: ek box hai, garam hoga, haath mat lagana. Hum kai din dhoondhte rahe. Kuch nahi mila.” He sipped his tea. “Raat ko dera mein ek saab baat kar rahe the. Bol rahe the: ho sakta hai barf usse kha gayi. Woh garam hai, toh barf pighlaata jayega, aur neeche, aur neeche.”

The sun was going down behind us. The snow on Nanda Devi turned from gold to pink to grey.

“Agle saal,” Dorje said, “doosre pahad pe, Nanda Kot pe, unhone aisa ek aur rakha tha. Phir laane gaye toh woh barf mein ek gufa bana ke baitha tha. Chaaron taraf barf pighli hui. Garam. Saal bhar baad bhi garam.”

He screwed the cap back onto his flask and turned to me, as if he knew which of us needed to hear it.

“Woh pahad kuch rakhta hai, beta. Usse jagaana nahi chahiye.”

Then he stood, nodded to us, and began to walk down in the dusk, slow and steady: an old man who had been walking these mountains for sixty years.

We sat on Chandrashila long after he’d gone. The stars came out, more stars than I’d seen in my whole life, so many that the sky looked crowded.

I did what I do. I opened my laptop. I read every document I could find, every declassified page, every article. I calculated half-lives. I’m not a nuclear physicist, but I know how to read and I know how to check numbers, and the numbers said this: the kind of plutonium in that device stays dangerous for a very, very long time. Longer than any of us will live. Longer than the songs.

“Rohan khazana nahi dhoondh raha,” I said at last. My voice sounded strange in all that silence.

Shaoni didn’t take her eyes off the peak. “Woh plutonium dhoondh raha hai.”

“Aur bechega.”

“Kisko?”

I didn’t answer her. None of the answers were good.

“Glacier pighal raha hai. Har saal thoda aur. Jo cheez saath saal se barf mein soyi hai—”

He stopped.

“—woh jaag rahi hai.”

Above us, the last light left Nanda Devi. Somewhere below, in a village we couldn’t see, the Pandav Lila drums went on and on, calling an old hero back into the world.

In my pocket, my phone buzzed once.

Unknown number. A photograph: a page from my own notebook, the one Rohan had taken at the Savoy, with a phone number written on it in my handwriting and one word next to it.

Mummy.

The number was circled. Twice.

Underneath:

Chapter Six

The Village That Never Forgave Hanuman

Day 6·Dronagiri

Before this chapter, reader, I need to confess something, so that you understand what it cost me.

I have recited the Hanuman Chalisa before every exam I have ever sat. School exams. University exams. My PhD viva, twice, once in the bathroom. I keep a small brass Hanuman on my desk at college, and my students know that if he’s facing the wall, they should not ask me for an extension.

And on Saturday morning, we drove to the one village in India that has never forgiven him.

It started the night before, in a cheap room in Chopta, with Shaoni pacing.

“Akele nahi jaoge,” she told me, for the ninth time. “Auli. Sunday. Akele. Yeh trap hai, Parth. Basic logic.”

“Toh kya karein?” I asked her.

She stopped pacing and tapped Kshitij’s diary, which was open on the bed between us.

“Usse pehle wahan pahuncho jahan woh abhi tak nahi pahuncha.” She put her finger on a name. “Pratap Singh Rawat. Dronagiri. Agar 1966 mein kisi ne garam shila sabse pehle dekhi thi, toh unke ghar mein koi na koi zaroor jaanta hoga.”

Kshitij looked up at her. “Aur agar Rohan bhi wahi soch raha ho?”

“Toh hum usse pehle pahunchenge,” said Shaoni. “Kal subah paanch baje.”

Gabbar, who had been half asleep in the plastic chair by the door, opened one eye. “Saade paanch, didi. Basanti ko bhi neend chahiye.”

So on Saturday we drove down from Chopta to the Alaknanda and up to Joshimath.

In Joshimath, Gabbar pulled Basanti up outside a tea stall with a tin roof and leaned on the horn. A man in his thirties came running out with a ladle still in his hand and threw his arms round Gabbar’s neck.

“Mahesh,” Gabbar announced to the three of us, unwinding himself, with the pride of a man introducing a minister. “Mere mama ka beta. Bachpan mein hum dono ek hi cycle pe school jaate the. Yeh chalata tha, main peeche baithta tha, aur jab bhi gire, galti meri hoti thi.”

“Abhi bhi teri hi hoti hai,” Mahesh told him, grinning.

He had a round, cheerful face, a cricket-bat sticker on the back of his phone, and the kind of energy that makes you buy a second chai you didn’t want. He ran this stall in the off-season, he told me, and a tea stall up at Auli in the ski season, and drove a taxi in between.

“Teen dhande, Professor sahab. Phir bhi EMI poori nahi hoti.” He laughed. “Joshimath mein daraar aayi, toh tourist bhi daraar dekh ke bhaag gaye.”

It was a joke. But his laugh stopped a little early.

When Gabbar told him we were looking for the family of Pratap Singh Rawat of Dronagiri, Mahesh knew exactly who we should ask: an old woman named Kamla Devi, the last person in the village who remembered Pratap. He drew us a map on a paper napkin. Then he asked Gabbar where we’d be staying that night, and for how long, and what exactly we were looking for. Friendly questions. The questions a cousin asks.

Gabbar answered all of them. Of course he did. You don’t keep secrets from the boy who pedalled you to school.

Kshitij wrote the name of the stall in his diary, out of habit. I remember thinking that was a little paranoid of him.

From Joshimath we drove east into the Dhauliganga valley, the road narrowing and climbing until it gave up entirely at a place called Jumma. From there, you walk.

The path to Dronagiri climbs through birch and pine and then out above the trees onto a wide, bare shelf of high pasture, ringed by mountains so close and so white they look as if they’ve been cut out of paper. On the far side of the valley, one mountain stood a little apart from the others. Its right flank looked… wrong. Not broken exactly. Scooped. As if a giant had taken a bite out of it.

Gabbar stopped on the path and pointed. “Dronagiri Parvat,” he told us quietly. “Inka devta.”

I stopped walking. So did Shaoni.

You know the story, I think. Everyone in India knows the story. In the Ramayana, Lakshman lies dying on the battlefield of Lanka, struck by Meghnad’s weapon. The only cure is a herb called Sanjeevani, which grows on a mountain in the Himalaya. Hanuman flies north to fetch it. But when he reaches the mountain, he can’t tell which plant is the right one. So, as every comic book of my childhood showed in glorious colour, he lifts the entire mountain and carries it to Lanka.

Heroic. Devoted. A story I have loved since I was four years old.

Now I was standing in the village that lost that mountain.

Dronagiri was half-empty when we arrived. It was the end of October, and the village was doing what it does every year before the snow: packing up. The families here are Bhotiya, high-mountain people who spend the summer up here with their flocks and fields and move down to warmer villages for the winter. Doors were being padlocked. Goats were being counted. An old man was tying a tin trunk onto a mule with the concentration of a surgeon.

The woman Mahesh had told us to find was sitting on a stone platform in the middle of all this, shelling beans, as if leaving for winter was a minor inconvenience she had survived eighty times. Which she had.

Her name was Kamla Devi. Eighty-six. Small, sharp-eyed, wrapped in a black woollen pankhi, with silver at her nose and her ears and a look that went straight through all three of us in about half a second.

Gabbar crouched beside her and explained, in Garhwali, who we were and who we were looking for. She stopped shelling beans.

“Pratap,” she said, in Hindi, to me. “Meri bahan ka aadmi tha.”

But first, because this is a pahadi village and nothing happens before tea, she made us sit on the platform and put a pot on to boil. And while it boiled, Kshitij, who had seen the mountain too, asked her about Hanuman.

She told us. And reader, I’m going to tell it to you the way she told it, because it is her village’s story, and she was not joking.

The people of Dronagiri worship their mountain, she said. Not a god on the mountain: the mountain itself. Parvat Dev. When Hanuman came looking for the herb, the villagers didn’t help him; the mountain was theirs, and so were its herbs. So Hanuman disguised himself as a poor old sadhu, went to an old woman of the village, and begged. Time was running out, he said. Someone would die.

She felt sorry for him. She pointed.

And Hanuman didn’t take a herb. He tore away the whole side of the mountain and flew off with it.

“Dekh lo,” Kamla Devi told us, nodding across the valley at the scooped flank. “Aaj bhi.”

The village punished the old woman, she said. They cast her out. And from that day, the women of Dronagiri have not been allowed to take part in the worship of the mountain. On the day of the great puja to Parvat Dev, the women don’t even serve the men their food.

“Aur Hanuman ji?” I asked her. My voice came out smaller than I meant it to.

“Is gaon mein unki pooja nahi hoti,” she said simply. “Laal jhanda nahi lagta. Aur jab Ramlila hoti hai…” She spread her hands. “Hanuman nahi aate. Ram hain. Sita hain. Ravan hai. Hanuman nahi.”

Kshitij’s pen froze. “Ramlila… bina Hanuman ke?” he asked her. “Phir Lanka kaun jalaata hai?”

Kamla Devi looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed: a big, cackling, delighted laugh that made a passing goat jump.

“Beta, yeh hamara gaon hai. Hamari kahani.” She wagged a bean pod at him. “Humne usse maaf nahi kiya. Baaki duniya kare. Hum nahi.”

I sat there with my chai, a lifelong Hanuman bhakt in a village where Hanuman was the villain, and I learned something about faith I hadn’t known.

It can be local. It can belong to a place. The same story that gave me courage before every exam took something from these people. Both of us are right. That night, I wrote it in my notebook: Same god. Same story. Different side of the mountain.

I didn’t recite the Chalisa in Dronagiri. Not even silently. It wasn’t my village.

But I’ll admit that on the way out, I turned and looked back at the scooped mountain and said, very quietly, Sorry, Bajrangbali. Aaj aapka din nahi tha.

Then she told us about Pratap.

He had been a shepherd, a young man in 1966, she said: strong, quiet, the kind who could find a lost goat on a mountainside in fog. That spring, very early, before the snow had gone, he and two friends took a few animals up towards the high pastures below the great peaks, earlier than anyone usually went.

“Chhe din baad lautey,” she told us. “Aur kisi se baat nahi ki. Kisi se nahi. Mahino tak.”

“Phir?” Shaoni asked her softly.

“Phir ek din, Pratap ne geet bana diya. Pahadi log aise hi karte hain. Jo bol nahi sakte, woh gaa dete hain.”

She closed her eyes and sang.

We had the first four lines already, and she sang them. Then the two lines we’d heard from Bachi Ram ji in Maletha, about the stone sleeping in the snow. And then (Kshitij’s hand gripped my arm so hard it hurt) two lines none of us had ever heard:

Jo garam tha, woh barf ne liya,

jo thanda bacha, charwaha le chala —

Rajula ke desh, Malushahi ki raah…

She stopped.

“Aage?” Shaoni whispered to her.

Kamla Devi opened her eyes. “Aage nahi hai, beti. Pratap ne kabhi aage nahi gaaya. Main poochti thi. Woh bas hansta tha.”

I repeated it slowly, translating as I went, for myself as much as anyone. “What was warm, the snow took. What stayed cold, the shepherd carried away.” I turned to Shaoni. “Do cheezein. Ek garam. Ek thandi.”

“Aur thandi wali,” said Shaoni, “charwahe le gaye. Kahin. Rajula ke desh.”

Kshitij looked from one of us to the other. “Rajula kaun?”

Gabbar and Kamla Devi both turned and stared at him as if he’d asked who Ram was.

“Rajula-Malushahi,” Gabbar told him. “Kumaon ki sabse badi prem kahani. Ek ladki jo pahad paar karke apne raja ko dhoondhne chali gayi.”

“Aur uska desh kahaan hai?”

Gabbar opened his mouth. Then he frowned and closed it.

“Pakka nahi pata,” he admitted. “Kumaon mein kahin upar. Har gaon kehta hai, hamara tha.”

Kshitij wrote it down, circled it, and drew a large question mark beside it, the way he does when a clue is real but locked.

As we stood up to leave, I saw a white SUV parked far below at the road head at Jumma. And coming up the path, cheerfully filming the half-empty village and the scooped mountain, came Golu.

“GHOST FAMILY! Dronagiri, jahan Hanuman ji ko bhi entry nahi! Kya yahan unki aatma—”

Kamla Devi stood up.

She stood up slowly, one hand on the stone wall, and stared at him. Not at his ring light. At his cap first (the faded blue woollen cap) for a long, strange moment, the way you’d look at something that belonged to someone else. Then at his face. Her eyes narrowed. Her lips moved.

Then she spoke, very clearly, in the voice of an old woman recognising a child she had once scolded for stealing apricots.

“Gaurav?”

Golu froze.

It lasted only a second. Then he laughed (too loud, too bright) and called back to her, “Nahi, dadi! Main Golu! Delhi se! Like aur subscribe karna!” He turned round, still filming, walked very quickly back down the path towards Jumma, and didn’t look back once.

Kamla Devi watched him go. She didn’t say anything else. She just sat back down and picked up her beans.

But her hands, I noticed, were shaking.

We reached our guesthouse in Joshimath after dark, aching and silent, and walked into the small lobby with its plastic flowers and a TV showing a cricket match nobody was watching.

Someone was sitting in the armchair by the heater. Navy quarter-zip. Expensive watch. A half-drunk cup of tea on the table beside him.

In his lap, open, was a brown leather notebook with Sir, likhte rehna stamped inside the cover.

Rohan Sehgal looked up and smiled at me the way you smile at an old friend.

“‘Mummy says wear the sweater,’” he read aloud, in a warm, amused voice. “‘Mummy says don’t chase ghosts.’ ‘Mummy called four times today.’” He turned a page. “You write about her a lot, Professor. It’s adorable. Genuinely.”

Behind him, filling the doorway to the dining room, Bhalla stood with his arms folded.

Rohan closed my notebook gently and set it on his knee, squared to the edge.

“Let’s not do this the boring way,” he said, still to me. “Tomorrow. Auli. The top of the ski slope. Eleven o’clock. You bring my notebook, and whatever that lovely old lady in Dronagiri sang to you today. Yes, I know you went. I know everything you do about four hours after you do it.” He smiled again. “I’ll give you yours back. Mummy’s number, poetry and all. And then you go home and forget this, and I forget her.”

He stood, finished his tea, and walked past us to the door, so close I could smell his cologne. At the door, he paused and looked back.

“Oh, and Professor? Come alone.” His eyes moved to Shaoni. “Your friend is very clever with jackets. I’d rather not find out what she does with ski slopes.”

The door closed. Bhalla followed him out. Outside, an engine started.

Nobody moved for a long time. Then Shaoni said, very quietly, the question that had been sitting in all our heads since we’d walked in.

“Chaar ghante.” She looked at each of us in turn. “Usse kaise pata chala hum Dronagiri gaye?”

None of us looked at each other.

And all of us thought about a white SUV parked at Jumma.

The road up to Auli climbs in long switchbacks out of Joshimath, through cracked streets and past houses with tarpaulins over their walls. Gabbar drove the slow way, by road, and nobody spoke.

Auli in late October is a huge sloping meadow, gold-brown and dusted with the first snow, ringed by oak and fir. And across the valley, so close you feel you could throw a stone at it, stands the whole wall of the Garhwal Himalaya. Dronagiri. Kamet. Hathi Parvat.

And Nanda Devi.

I’d seen her from Chandrashila at sunset, small and far away. Here she filled half the sky: twin summits, white, sharp, impossibly high, with a thin plume of snow blowing off the top like breath.

Somewhere on her slopes, since 16 October 1965, a box full of plutonium had been sleeping.

I folded my hands. I didn’t decide to. They just did it.

At a tea stall near the top of the slope, while I waited for eleven o’clock, someone waved at me with a ladle.

Mahesh. He had come up early to open his ski-season stall, and the stall was already full of local men arguing, the way men at tea stalls everywhere are arguing, except that here they were arguing about a goddess.

“Barah saal mein ek baar hoti hai Raj Jat,” one of them was saying, banging his glass down. “Aur is saal do-do committee! Kurud wale September mein Homkund tak le gaye. Nauti wale bole, nahi, hamari Raj Jat 2027 mein hogi—”

The Nanda Devi Raj Jat: once every twelve years, the goddess Nanda is escorted from her parents’ home to her husband Shiva’s home on Kailash, on a three-week, 280-kilometre walk through the high mountains, led by a rare four-horned ram. In 2026, two committees couldn’t agree. One went ahead in September. The other postponed to next year. The valley was still arguing about it a month later.

“Bhagwan ke ghar mein bhi committee ki problem,” I said to the stall in general. “Very relatable. Hamare college mein bhi.”

I’ve read the same articles you read in that car.” Now he turned and looked at me, and for a second the charm slipped, and I saw what was underneath it. Not madness. Something worse. Calm. “Do you know what’s going to happen to that device? Nothing. The glacier will keep melting. One day a trekker, or a goat, or a flood, will bring it down. Into a village. Into a river. “To find it. What happens after—” he spread his hands “—is economics.”

Then a flood, a pandemic, a town that started sinking. Do you know what it’s like, Professor, to love something that keeps locking its door on you?”

“Summit Secrets has eleven crore of debt. My investors would like their money. And on that mountain there’s a box that some very serious people abroad would pay a great deal to hold.” He shrugged again. “The mountain owes me a key.”

Except one page, torn out neatly at the spine. The page with Mummy’s number on it.

That’s when we heard the buzzing.

Golu was standing at the edge of the slope a hundred metres away, flying a drone. He hadn’t spoken to any of us all morning. After Dronagiri, after Gaurav, he’d kept his distance: sulking, Kshitij thought; hiding, Shaoni thought. I stood up and shouted across the snow at him. “Golu! Bhai! Delete karo! Woh recording—”

Then it stopped buzzing.

Golu stared after it. “Battery,” he said, to nobody. “Thand mein battery mar gayi.”

I turned to the reader. I’m turning to you now.

“Another device,” I said, “lost on Nanda Devi. It’s basically a tradition.”

Kshitij insisted we dig it out. He said it to all of us, but he was looking at Golu.

“Footage. Data. Kuch bhi ho sakta hai usme.”

It took forty minutes. Golu went first, wading knee-deep through the snow up the slope behind the meadow, Shaoni behind him, me behind her, wheezing complaints about the author of this chapter. We found the drone at last, buried nose-first in a drift on a ridge, cold as a stone.

Golu didn’t want to show us the footage. That was the first strange thing. He held the drone against his chest and said the memory card was probably broken. Shaoni simply held out her hand, palm up, and waited until he gave it to her.

We watched it on my laptop in the back of Basanti, with the heater roaring.

The meadow. The bench. Rohan and me, two tiny figures. Then the drone climbing and banking (not drifting, I noticed now, but turning smoothly, precisely, as if following a route) and gliding over the ridge behind Auli into a hidden hollow in the hills that you could never see from the slope.

And in the hollow: tents.

Four big orange expedition tents, the kind that cost more than my car. Ropes. Ice axes stacked against a rock. Two men loading sledges. A machine on wheels that looked like a lawnmower crossed with a radar (“Ground-penetrating radar,” Kshitij said; he’d seen one in a documentary). And, being carried carefully between two men towards the biggest tent, a heavy grey metal case.

The drone dipped lower just before its battery died. For three frames, the case filled the screen.

Stencilled on its side in yellow was a symbol every person on earth recognises, even if they don’t know they do.

Three black blades around a black dot.

Radiation.

Nobody in the car said anything.

And then, for the first time on this entire trip, my day job turned out to be useful.

Because a drone doesn’t only keep video. Next to every clip on that memory card sat a small, boring text file: the flight log. Every second of the flight is written down there: latitude, longitude, altitude, speed, battery, and the mode the drone was flying in. Nobody looks at those files. I’m a developer. I look at boring files for fun.

I opened it in a code editor, wrote four lines of Python with my fingers still freezing, and plotted the flight path on a map.

It wasn’t a drift. It was a straight line from the meadow to the hollow, then a neat, tight rectangle over the camp (a survey pattern), then a straight line back. And in the mode column, from the very first second after take-off, the same word repeated eleven hundred times.

WAYPOINT.

“Yeh drone khud nahi gaya wahan,” I said. My voice sounded very small. “Iska route pehle se set tha. Ghar pe baithke, map pe. Kisi ko pata tha camp kahan hai, aur usne isse wahan bheja tha.”

Kshitij leaned over my shoulder, read the screen, and wrote down every coordinate. Then he looked at me with an expression I will treasure for the rest of my life.

“Bhai,” he said. “Aaj aapne notes banaye.”

Then all four of us turned, at the same moment, to look at Golu.

But Golu was no longer standing outside the car.

His white SUV was already halfway down the road to Joshimath.

Who Brought the Greeks to Skeleton Lake?

Day 8·Wan and Roopkund

The first thing I did, back in Joshimath, was call my mother.

“Mummy, ek kaam karo. Ek hafte ke liye Vadodara chali jao. Mausi ke ghar.”

Silence. Then, with the deadly precision of every Indian mother who has ever lived:

“Bhoot ke peeche pade ho na?”

“Mummy—”

“Maine kaha tha. Pehle din kaha tha.” A pause. “Sweater pehna?”

“Teen.”

“Theek hai. Jaa rahi hoon. Mausi ko bata dena tum kitne buddhu ho.” Another pause. Then, softer: “Parth. Dhyaan rakhna.”

I’d like to report that I was completely calm during this conversation. Shaoni, who was sitting on the other bed listening, would like to report otherwise. We’ve agreed not to discuss it.

Then Kshitij found the problem.

He was sitting cross-legged on the floor with my laptop, going through the photos I’d taken of Rohan’s notebook at the Savoy (every page, backed up in the folder called Unit 4 Practical Marks), looking for anything we’d missed. Suddenly he stopped scrolling and turned the screen towards us.

On a page near the back, in a list of crossed-out village names, was a line we had all skimmed past.

Wan — Bhawan Singh — grandson? porter. Check.

“Bhawan Singh,” Kshitij said. “Bahi mein teesra naam. Pratap Singh Rawat, Bhawan Singh, Kundan Singh.”

Wan is the last village before the trail to Roopkund.

I felt the floor tilt. “Toh humne usse jo jhootha verse diya—”

“—usne usse bilkul sahi jagah bhej diya.” Shaoni finished the sentence for me, grimly. “Murdon ki jheel ke raaste mein ek asli aadmi baitha hai, jiske dada ne 1966 mein garam shila dekhi thi. Aur Rohan wahan ja raha hai, yeh sochke ki geet use wahan bula raha hai.”

We’d tried to send a wolf into an empty field. We had sent him straight to a sheep.

Kshitij had gone white. He was staring at the screen as if it had bitten him.

“Meri galti hai,” he said. “Maine verse likha. Maine notebook dhyaan se nahi padha. Main clue finder hoon aur maine—”

“Hamari,” said Shaoni.

“Didi, maine—”

“Plan mera tha. Verse tumhara. Notebook Parth ne churaya. Gaadi Gabbar bhaiya chala rahe the.” She ticked it off on her fingers, looking at her brother the whole time. “Galti sabki hai, toh galti kisi ki nahi hai. Logic.” She picked up her bag. “Ab chalo. Rona hai toh Wan pahunch ke.”

It wasn’t logic, of course. It was a sister. But Kshitij stood up.

Gabbar was already starting Basanti.

It’s a long drive from Joshimath to Wan: down to Karnaprayag, up the Pindar valley, through villages hanging off the hillsides like washing, and finally up a narrow road to a village of slate roofs and walnut trees at the edge of the forest. We drove most of the night.

Bhawan Singh’s grandson was called Hira. Forty-something, wiry, with a face burned dark by sun at altitude and hands like old rope. He’d been a porter on the Roopkund trail since he was fifteen.

And two days earlier, he had been hired by a company called Summit Secrets.

He told us this sitting on his doorstep at dawn with a cup of tea, not quite looking at any of us.

“Bahut paisa de rahe hain. Season khatam ho gaya hai. Itni barf mein koi upar nahi jaata. Par yeh log jaana chahte hain. Kehte hain koi survey hai. Aaj subah ka plan tha. Phir unhone kaha, ruk jao, saab khud aa rahe hain.”

Shaoni sat down on the step below him. “Hira ji,” she asked carefully, “aapke dada ne kabhi ek garam pathar ke baare mein baat ki thi?”

Hira looked up then. Really looked at us, for the first time.

“Aap logon ko kaise pata?”

We told him. Not everything. Enough.

He listened, turning his cup round and round. Then he said something that changed the shape of the whole story.

“Dada kehte the: hum Roopkund ke raaste wapas aaye the. Wahan se nahi.” He shook his head. “Woh pathar Roopkund mein nahi tha. Woh bahut upar tha. Bade pahad ke paas. Woh log teen din chale the barf mein. Aur wapas aate waqt, raat ko, Roopkund ke paas ruk gaye the. Bas.”

Kshitij leaned forward. “Aur teesra charwaha? Kundan Singh?”

“Kundan chacha?” Hira almost smiled. “Unhone baad mein shaadi kar li. Kumaon mein kahin. Dada hamesha ek hi baat kehte the: Kundan Rajula ke desh chala gaya, aur thandi cheez le gaya.”

Kshitij’s pen moved so fast it tore the page.

Thandi cheez. What stayed cold, the shepherd carried away. To Rajula’s country.

It wasn’t a riddle any more. It was a man. A real man named Kundan Singh, who had married into a family somewhere in the high villages of Kumaon sixty years ago and taken something with him.

That’s when the engines came up the road.

Two dark SUVs. Rohan got out of the first, wearing a jacket that probably had its own embassy. Bhalla got out of the second, with three more men and a stack of gear.

Rohan saw us on Hira’s doorstep and stopped. For one second, his face did nothing at all. Then he smiled at me.

“Professor. You do get around.”

He didn’t ask why we were there. He didn’t need to. He turned to Hira and spoke pleasantly, as if the rest of us weren’t there.

“Hira ji. We’re leaving in twenty minutes.” Then, with a glance back at us: “Your friends are welcome to come along, if they can keep up.”

It was an invitation the way a tiger’s yawn is an invitation.

Hira looked at us. We looked at Hira. And Shaoni answered Rohan, very calmly, before I could.

“Hum aa rahe hain.”

I wanted to ask her what on earth she was thinking. I didn’t, because I already knew. Hira was going up a mountain in the snow with a man who had threatened a ninety-one-year-old in Maletha and my mother in Ahmedabad. We weren’t letting him go alone.

And somewhere at the back of my mind, I couldn’t stop hearing Rohan on the bench at Auli. I used to work with a porter. Young boy. Brilliant on ice.

The trail to Roopkund goes up through oak forest to the meadows of Ali and Bedni Bugyal: vast, rolling, golden in autumn, with the whole wall of Trishul shining above. In summer, I’m told, it’s one of the most beautiful walks in India. In late October, with Rohan Sehgal walking twenty metres behind me, it was one of the longest.

We camped at Bhagwabasa, a bleak, stony hollow at about 4,000 metres. In the dark, Rohan’s men set up their big orange tents among the rocks and talked in low voices, and Bhalla sat outside all night, facing the dark, never sleeping.

Around midnight, one of Rohan’s younger men got bored and started whistling a tune by the stove: a pahadi tune, the kind every porter knows. Bhalla was on his feet before the third note. He didn’t shout. He just put one huge hand on the young man’s shoulder and said, quietly, “Yahan nahi.”

Not here.

The young man stopped. Bhalla sat back down and stared into the dark towards the north, where the big mountains were, and didn’t move again until dawn.

Kshitij watched him over the edge of his sleeping bag. In the torchlight I saw him write: Bhalla is afraid of something on this mountain. More than of us. Not “here”. HERE. Has he been up here before?

We set off again at four in the morning, by torchlight, up a steep, icy zigzag towards the lake.

I’d read about Roopkund. Everyone who has ever googled “mysterious places in India” has. But nothing prepares you for actually standing at the rim at dawn, at more than 5,000 metres, with your lungs full of broken glass, and looking down.

It’s small. That’s the first shock. Not a great lake: a round, dark bowl of water, half-frozen, ringed by scree and old snow, cupped in the mountain below the ridge of Junargali.

And around its edges, among the stones and half-buried in snow, there are pale shapes that you slowly realise are bones.

Hundreds of people died here. Their remains have lain scattered around this lake for as long as anyone can remember. For decades, nobody knew who they were. Then, in 2019, scientists published the results of a long DNA study of thirty-eight of the skeletons in the journal Nature Communications: the first ancient DNA ever reported from India.

The skeletons didn’t belong to one group who died in one disaster. They belonged to at least three distinct groups, who died here at different times, roughly a thousand years apart. Most had ancestry typical of South Asia. But one group of fourteen had ancestry from the eastern Mediterranean (people closely related to those of Greece and Crete), and they had died here around 1800.

Greeks. At a lake 5,000 metres up in the Garhwal Himalaya. Nobody knows why they came. Nobody knows how they died.

Hira, standing beside me, told me the valley’s answer, which is older than DNA. Roopkund lies on the route of the Nanda Devi Raj Jat, he said. Long ago, a king set out on the pilgrimage with a grand party (servants, musicians, dancers), and they broke the goddess’s rules on her sacred path. Nanda’s anger came down on them as a storm of hail, and they died here, on the shore of this lake, and their bones never left.

I did what I do. I folded my hands. Science on one side. Story on the other. A lake full of strangers in the middle, who had travelled from the ends of the earth to die in the snow in a place they had no reason to be.

My phone buzzed. One bar of signal. Of course. Of course it was.

Mummy.

“Pahunch gaye mausi ke ghar,” she told me. “Tum kahan ho?”

“Ek jheel pe, Mummy.”

“Achha hai. Kuch garam khaya?”

I looked down at the bones.

“Nahi, Mummy,” I said. “Haddiyan gin raha hoon.”

There was a long pause from Vadodara.

“Parth,” said my mother, “tum ghar aao. Phir baat karenge.”

Rohan’s men had dragged their radar machine up on a sledge. For two hours they ran it along the lake’s edge, back and forth across the scree, while Rohan stood on a rock and watched the screen of a rugged tablet.

Nothing. Rock. Ice. Bone.

I saw the moment he understood. He didn’t shout. He didn’t swear. He simply lowered the tablet and stood very still, looking at the lake.

Then he turned and looked at Kshitij.

Not at me. Not at Shaoni. At Kshitij: the quiet one with the diary, who had spent the whole morning writing and had never once looked surprised.

“Murdon ki jheel,” Rohan said softly, to him. “Haddiyan gin le.” He almost laughed. “That’s good. That’s genuinely good. You wrote that, didn’t you?”

Kshitij didn’t answer. But his ears went red.

“Clever boy,” said Rohan. For the first time, there was no warmth in his voice at all. He turned his back on us. “Bhalla. Hum neeche chal rahe hain.”

The way down from Roopkund to Bhagwabasa crosses a long, steep traverse under a wall of loose rock: the kind of slope where the stones are held in place only by the cold.

By then, the sun was full on it.

We were strung out in a line. Hira first, then me, then Kshitij. Shaoni a little behind us, keeping an eye on Bhalla, who had fallen back to walk near her. Rohan and his men were ahead of all of us.

I heard it before I saw it: a dry crack high above, like a gunshot. Then a sound like gravel being poured into a giant’s bucket.

Hira spun round and screamed at all of us. “Bhaago!”

I grabbed Kshitij’s jacket and ran. Stones went past me: small at first, then bigger, bouncing and spinning and smashing into the path behind us. A rock the size of a pressure cooker hit the ground a metre from my foot and leapt over my head into the valley. Dust everywhere. I couldn’t see. I couldn’t breathe. I just held on to Kshitij and ran until Hira grabbed us both and pulled us down behind a boulder.

And then, slowly, it stopped.

The dust drifted away on the wind.

Where the path had been behind us, there was now a raw, fresh slope of broken rock, still shifting, still clicking and settling. It had cut the trail cleanly in two.

On our side: Hira. Me. Kshitij.

On the far side, forty metres away across the slide, two figures stood alone on the trail, covered in grey dust.

Shaoni.

And Bhalla.

She looked across at us. She raised one hand: I’m okay. Then she turned and looked at the big man beside her, and he looked at her, and between them and us lay forty metres of rock that nobody on earth could cross.

Rohan’s voice came down the slope from above, calm as ever.

“Bhalla! Ladki ke saath raho. Hum neeche milte hain.”

The sun went behind a cloud. And Shaoni, without another glance at us, started walking down the far side of the mountain, with Bhalla one step behind her.

Kshitij didn’t move. He stood there with his diary clutched against his chest and watched his sister until she was too small to see.

“Tab maine socha,” Shaoni went on. “Logic. Yeh aadmi kis cheez se darta hai? Hum se nahi. Rohan se nahi. Andhere se. Pahad ki raat se.” She looked down at her hands. “Aur andhere mein koi hai, jisse woh maafi maang raha hai.”

So she waited until about one in the morning. Then she crawled out of the back of the tent, very slowly, moved twenty metres away among the boulders, keeping low, and called his name.

Softly. Long. The way Gabbar had told us Khabees calls a traveller in the forest.

“Bhal-laaa…”

Bhalla stood up.

“Bhal-laaa…”

He swung the torch round. He didn’t call back. He didn’t take a single step towards the voice. He did exactly what every child in these mountains is told to do: he didn’t answer, and he didn’t follow.

Instead, he went into the tent and zipped it shut.

“Aur maine charwahon wala raasta liya,” Shaoni finished. “Hira bhaiya ne subah dikhaya tha. Chaar ghante. Neeche aa gayi.”

There was a long silence in the car.

Kshitij broke it, very quietly. “Didi. Aapne ek Khabees ki tarah ek gunde ko darr ke tent mein band kar diya.”

“Logic,” said Shaoni.

“Tilu Rauteli,” said Gabbar, from the driver’s seat.

“Bhoot,” I said. For once, nobody corrected me.

Much later that day, when Kshitij had fallen asleep against the window and Gabbar was concentrating on the hairpins, Shaoni leaned forward between the seats and spoke to me, very quietly, without looking at me.

“Parth. Kal raat. Jab main pathron ke beech chhupi thi aur uska naam le rahi thi.” She paused. “Main bahut darr gayi thi.”

In all the years I’ve known her, she has never said that sentence. I didn’t make a joke. I’m learning.

“Pata hai,” I told her. “Isliye toh tum Tilu ho. Bina darr ke koi Tilu nahi hota. Bas lambi hoti hai.”

She snorted and sat back. And for the rest of the drive over the pass, she kept her headlamp switched on in her lap, in broad daylight, and neither of us mentioned it.

We needed somewhere to disappear. Gabbar had already decided where.

“Meri Ija ka gaon,” he told me, eyes on the road. “Bageshwar ke upar. Wahan koi Basanti ko nahi dhoondhega.” He glanced across at me. “Aur aaj raat wahan jagar hai. Woh ladka. Yaad hai?”

I remembered. Chapter 2. The dhaba fire. Gaon mein jagar hone wala hai. Ek ladke ke liye. Bujurg kehte hain usse Masan ne pakda hai. And Gabbar saying: Maine kaha, season hai.

He wasn’t saying season now.

We drove out of Garhwal over the pass at Gwaldam and down into Kumaon, and the hills changed: softer, greener, pine giving way to deodar, the houses painted bright blue and pink with carved wooden doorways. Gabbar’s mother’s village sat on a ridge above the Saryu valley, a cluster of slate-roofed houses around a temple and a big stone courtyard.

Gabbar’s mother (everyone, including Gabbar, called her Ija) was a tiny woman in a green cardigan, and she was standing in her doorway before Basanti had even stopped, as if she’d heard the engine from three valleys away.

Gabbar got out of the car. He’s a big man, Gabbar. Fifty years old, a moustache like a broom, thirty years of mountain roads in his hands. He walked up the path to his mother like a boy who’d broken a window.

“Do saal,” Ija said to him.

“Ija—”

She reached up (she had to reach a long way) and smacked him once on the side of the head, not hard, the way you’d knock on a door. Then she pulled his face down to hers with both hands and held it there for a long moment, her eyes closed.

Then she let go, looked past him at the three of us standing awkwardly by the car, and said, “Sab kamzor dikh rahe ho.” And she fed us until we couldn’t move.

She put turmeric on Shaoni’s scratch without asking. She put a woollen cap on Kshitij’s head without asking. She looked at me for a while and then, without asking, gave me a second helping of bhatt ki churkani, which is the Kumaoni version of a hug.

Halfway through lunch, Gabbar’s phone buzzed, and he stepped out into the courtyard with it, smiling. Through the window I heard him: “Haan, pahunch gaye. Ija ke paas. Haan, khana kha rahe hain, bhatt ki churkani. Haan, tere liye bhi bachaungi bolti hai, aa ja kabhi…” And he laughed, the easy laugh of a man talking to family.

I didn’t think about it. Why would I? I was eating bhatt ki churkani.

Gabbar came back in and sat down beside his mother. She put a hand on his knee and left it there for the rest of the meal, and he let her. I don’t think he had let anyone do that in thirty years.

I fell asleep in her kitchen in the afternoon and woke up with a blanket over me and the sound of drums being tuned in the courtyard.

Reader.

From here until the end of this chapter, there are no jokes.

I told you in the Author’s Note that when the drums start, the jokes stop. I meant it as a rule then. Tonight it is something else. I’m a believer. I want to tell you what I saw the way you’d tell someone about a puja in your own home: carefully, and with my hands folded.

Jagar comes from jaga, to wake.

It is a night-long ritual of the Garhwal and Kumaon hills, and its purpose is exactly what its name says: to wake the gods. The local deities (the gods of a family, a village, a valley) are believed to sleep. In a Jagar they are called back, invited to come down into the world for one night, and asked for help. Healing. Justice. An answer to a question nobody else can answer.

It happens at night, in the home of the family who needs help, and it needs two people above all.

The jagariya is the singer. Tonight he was an old man with white stubble and a wool waistcoat, sitting cross-legged on a blanket in the courtyard with a hurka, a small hourglass-shaped drum, under one arm. Beside him sat two younger men with a big brass thali and a stick, and later the dhol and damau. The jagariya sings the god’s story from the very beginning, in long verses, the whole life of the deity, and the drums carry it.

The dangariya is the one the god comes into. Tonight it was a quiet, middle-aged man in a white kurta, a farmer from the next village, sitting very still on the other side of the fire with his eyes closed. In Kumaoni, Gabbar whispered to me, dangar means a way, a path. The dangariya is the path the god walks down.

The family sat close: mother, father, grandmother. And the boy, thin, about fourteen, wrapped in a shawl, with dark circles under his eyes. Ija told me very softly that he’d been sick for two months. Fevers. Nightmares. Talking in his sleep. Doctors in Bageshwar and Haldwani hadn’t found anything. He had fallen ill after walking home late, alone, past the cremation ground by the river.

The elders said Masan had caught him.

I’m a professor. I could give you three medical explanations for what Ija described. I was also sitting in her courtyard. So I’ll tell you only what happened.

The jagariya began just after dark.

He started slowly, the hurka ticking under his fingers like a heartbeat, his voice rising and falling in long lines of old Kumaoni that I couldn’t follow word for word but didn’t need to. Gabbar leaned close and whispered the outline to me.

He was singing of Golu Devta, Kumaon’s god of justice: the son of Jhal Rai, a king of Champawat, and his youngest queen, Kalinka. The jealous older queens who stole the newborn and put a stone in his place, and threw the child into the river. The fisherman who found him. The boy who grew up and rode a wooden horse to the palace and asked the queens whether a wooden horse could drink water, and, when they laughed, asked them whether a queen could give birth to a stone. The king who finally understood. The boy who became the god people go to when there is nobody else to go to.

Then the jagariya sang of Gangnath: a prince of Doti who left his kingdom for love of a woman named Bhana and was murdered for it. And how, after his death, his restless spirit troubled the people, until they began to worship him (him and Bhana together), and the trouble stopped. A ghost who became a god. Kshitij wrote that line down twice.

Gabbar sat beside Ija on the blanket the whole night, cross-legged, his topi in his lap. I watched him more than I watched the fire. He knew the tunes (you could see it in the way his head moved) but not the words; he’d mouth a line, lose it, and find it again two lines later. Once, when the jagariya reached a verse about a son who leaves home for the plains and forgets the songs of his village, I saw Ija’s hand find his on the blanket. Neither of them looked at the other.

Hour after hour. The drums grew louder. The dhol and damau joined in. The thali rang. The fire threw sparks into a sky so crowded with stars it looked like spilled salt.

And at the very back of the courtyard, in the shadow of the temple wall, I noticed someone I hadn’t seen arrive.

Golu.

No ring light. No camera. No phone in his hand at all. Just a man in a puffer jacket and an old blue cap, standing absolutely still, listening. He saw me see him. He didn’t look away. He didn’t move.

I didn’t say anything. Not here. Not tonight.

It was a little after three in the morning when the dangariya began to shake.

It started in his hands. Then his shoulders. The drums went faster. The jagariya’s voice climbed and climbed, calling, calling. The dangariya’s whole body began to tremble, his head rolling back, his breath coming in short hard gasps. Then he rose to his knees, and then, with the drums at their peak, he let out a cry that went straight through my ribs and stopped everything.

The drums fell silent all at once.

In the silence, the dangariya spoke. His voice was deeper than before. Steady. Calm. Not his own.

The family bowed. The grandmother wept. The father, his hands folded, began to speak, quietly, in Kumaoni, about his son. The god listened. Then the god answered, for a long time, and I don’t know what he said, and it isn’t mine to know. Ija told me later only this: that a Masan Puja would be done for the boy at the river, and that the family should be at peace.

Then the dangariya turned his head.

He looked across the fire. Past the family. Past Ija. At me.

I don’t know how to describe the feeling of being looked at like that. As if every stupid thing I had ever done and every true thing I had ever thought were laid out on a table, and someone was reading them, slowly, without judgement.

And the god spoke, in Hindi, clearly enough for all of us to hear.

“Jo pahad ne liya hai, use wapas mat maango.”

What the mountain has taken, do not ask for it back.

Then his eyes closed, and he sank down, and the drums began again, softly, and the god went home.

I didn’t move for a long time. Nobody near me did.

When I finally turned my head, I saw Golu at the back wall.

He was crying.

Not loudly. Not like a man who wanted anyone to see. Silent tears, running down his face and into his collar, his jaw clamped shut, his eyes fixed on the place where the dangariya had sat. A man who had just heard something he’d needed to hear, or had been afraid to hear, for a very long time.

Kshitij saw it too. He didn’t write anything. For the first time in this entire journey, he closed his diary and just watched.

I’ll tell you what I thought, sitting by that dying fire at four in the morning in a village above the Saryu. I thought about Rohan at Auli: I used to work with a porter. Young boy. Brilliant on ice. He liked old songs. I thought about Kamla Devi in Dronagiri, saying Gaurav? to a YouTuber who ran. And I thought about a sentence spoken in a god’s voice (what the mountain has taken) and who in that courtyard it might have been meant for.

I didn’t put the thoughts together. Not yet. I was too tired, and too moved, and too much of a fool.

But I’m telling you now, reader, so you can’t say I didn’t warn you.

The jokes are back on. You may stretch your legs.

At dawn, as the family took the boy down to the river, Gabbar’s phone buzzed.

He looked at it and went very still. Then he turned the screen round and held it out to me.

Unknown number.

Bahut sundar jagar tha. Ija ji ka khana bhi bahut tareef laayak hai. Subah milte hain. — R.

Nobody had told Rohan we were here. Nobody could have. Gabbar hadn’t told anyone except his own family, and…

Gabbar’s face went grey.

Shaoni turned, slowly, to look at the back wall of the courtyard.

Golu was gone.

Chapter Ten

Dear God, Please Find Attached

Day 10·Chitai

We were packed and in Basanti in eleven minutes. I have never seen four people move that fast, and I have seen students leave a lecture hall when the bell rings early.

Ija walked us down to the car in the grey dawn with a shawl over her head and pressed a steel tiffin into Kshitij’s hands. As Gabbar started the engine, Kshitij leaned out of the back window and asked her the question he’d written down the night before.

“Ija ji. Aapka maayka. Kaunsa gaon?”

She looked surprised to be asked. Then she smiled the faraway smile people get when they talk about the house they were born in.

“Johar mein, beta. Bahut upar. Milam ke raaste mein. Ab wahan sardiyon mein koi nahi rehta. Sab neeche aa jaate hain.”

“Aur aapki maa ne woh geet… garam pathar wala… kahaan se seekha tha?”

“Mausa ji se.” Ija shrugged, as if it were obvious. “Meri mausi ne ek Garhwali charwahe se shaadi ki thi. Dronagiri ki taraf ka. Kundan naam tha. Wahi gaate the.”

Gabbar slammed on the brakes so hard we all hit the seats in front of us.

He twisted round in the driver’s seat and stared out of the window at his mother.

“Kundan? Ija, aapne kabhi bataya nahi—”

“Tune kabhi poocha nahi,” said Ija, entirely reasonably. “Tu toh bachpan mein sirf gaadiyon ki baat karta tha.”

I’m not going to tell you what Gabbar’s face did. He’s a proud man, and he’s going to read this book.

“Kundan mausa ab nahi rahe,” Ija went on, to Kshitij, who had his pen out. “Bahut saal hue. Unke bete ki bahu hai, Dhana. Sardiyon mein Gangolihat ke paas rehti hai. Garmiyon mein Johar wale ghar jaati hai. Purane ghar mein mausa ka saara saamaan rakha hai. Koi haath nahi lagata.”

Then she patted Gabbar’s cheek through the window and told him to drive slowly. She told Shaoni to put more turmeric on her face. She told me I was too thin for a professor. And she walked back up the path to her house.

We sat in the stopped car in silence.

“Johar,” Gabbar said finally. His voice was strange. “Rajula bhi Johar ki thi. Shauka ladki. Johar ki. Sab yahi kehte hain.”

Rajula ke desh.

In the back seat, Kshitij closed his eyes, opened his diary, wrote one word in capitals and underlined it three times.

JOHAR.

We needed to think. We needed somewhere Rohan wouldn’t expect. And, if I’m honest, I needed somewhere I could ask for help from someone more powerful than a professor with a laptop.

So we went to complain to a god.

The temple of Golu Devta at Chitai sits in a grove of trees a few kilometres from Almora, at the end of a path lined with sweet shops and bell sellers. You hear it before you see it.

Bells.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Hanging from every beam, every rail, every branch, every wire strung between the trees. Brass bells as small as your thumb and bells as big as a bucket, old green ones and shining new ones, tied there by people from every corner of Kumaon and far beyond. When the wind moves through the grove, they all speak at once, softly, like a crowd murmuring.

And in between the bells: paper.

Letters. Petitions. Stamp papers. Notebook pages. Some in careful Hindi, some in English, some in Kumaoni, some in shaky pencil. Folded, rolled, pinned, tied with red thread to every surface that would hold them.

This is how you talk to Golu Devta, the god of justice. You write to him. You state your case. You ask for justice: against a cheating landlord, a corrupt official, an unfair boss, an illness, a lost child, a lost job. And if your wish is granted, you come back and tie a bell.

The god we had heard sung about all night (the boy swapped for a stone, who rode a wooden horse into his father’s palace) runs the oldest and busiest complaints department in the Himalaya.

I stood in the middle of all those bells, and I’ll tell you the truth: I laughed. Not at the god. With relief. Because for the first time in ten days, I had come somewhere where nobody expected a professor to have the answers.

I read the petitions. You’re allowed to; they hang right there in the open. And they broke my heart a little, one by one.

Golu Devta, mere bete ki naukri lagwa do. Teen saal se ghar baitha hai.

Hey Nyaya Devta, hamare khet par padosi ne kabza kar liya hai. Court mein das saal ho gaye.

Meri maa ka operation hai 14 tarikh ko. Unhe theek kar dena.

I am writing this from Bangalore. My mother tied a bell here for me when I was born. Please let me come home soon.

I had come looking for a mystery. I found a village noticeboard for the whole of human trouble. Jobs. Land. Sickness. Distance. Fear. Every one of them signed with a real name, by someone who had climbed up here believing that somebody, somewhere, was listening.

That’s the hidden Uttarakhand nobody puts on a postcard, reader. Not the ghosts. Not the plutonium. This.

I bought a sheet of stamp paper from a shop at the gate. I sat down on a step. And I wrote my own petition, properly, in the format I’ve taught for twelve years, because I am who I am.

To, Shri Golu Devta ji, Nyaya ke Devta, Chitai, Almora.

Subject: Request for protection from one Mr Rohan Sehgal and assistance regarding a missing nuclear device.

Respected Sir,

With due respect, I wish to state that I am a professor from Ahmedabad, currently being chased across your jurisdiction by a man who wants to dig up something that should stay buried. He has my mother’s phone number. He has kidnapped my friend’s brother (temporarily, with chai). He threatened a 91-year-old man in Maletha. Kindly do the needful.

Thanking you in anticipation, Yours faithfully, Prof. Parth (Unknown Entity, Female?)

I rolled it up and tied it to the railing with red thread, next to a petition about a stolen buffalo. Then I folded my hands and meant every word.

“Tum bhi likho,” I said to Shaoni.

“Data nahi hai,” she told me. “Ek god of justice ka complaint department. Response time? Success rate? Koi SLA?” She shook her head. “Main professional hoon, Parth.”

Five minutes later, when she thought we were all looking at the bells, Kshitij nudged me with his elbow and tilted his head.

At the far end of the railing, Shaoni was standing alone, tying a single red thread to the iron. No paper. No petition. Just the thread, knotted three times, very neatly, the way she does everything.

She folded her hands for exactly three seconds. Then she walked back to us with her logic face on, and Kshitij and I became extremely interested in a bell shaped like a pressure cooker.

Gabbar didn’t write anything either. He stood in front of the god for a long time with his topi in his hands, his lips moving. When he came back, his eyes were red, and he told me it was the incense.

“Achha format hai,” said a voice behind me. “Bas Kindly do the needful thoda sarkari lagta hai.”

An old man was standing beside me at the railing, tying a small brass bell next to my petition. Tall, straight-backed, white moustache trimmed with military precision, a tweed cap and a walking stick he clearly didn’t need.

“Mannat poori hui?” I asked him, nodding at the bell.

“Pote ka admission. IIT.” He gave the bell a little flick so that it rang. “Golu Devta ne suna. Ab main bhi kuch sun leta hoon.” He nodded at my petition. “Maaf karna, main padh raha tha. Purani aadat.” His eyebrows rose a fraction. “Missing nuclear device.”

His name was Bhupal Singh Dhami. Retired Commandant, Indo-Tibetan Border Police. Thirty-four years on the borders of Kumaon and Garhwal.

We sat on the temple steps and I told him everything. Shaoni showed him the drone frames on her phone: the hidden camp above Auli, the radar, the grey case with the radiation symbol. Kshitij showed him the diary.

He listened without interrupting, the way Inspector Bisht had. But at the end, he didn’t laugh.

“1965 ki baat sab jaante hain jo upar rahe hain,” he told me quietly. “Hum log baat nahi karte. Par sab jaante hain.” He looked at the drone frame for a long time. “Yeh case mazaak nahi hai, beta. Yeh asli shielding container hai.”

Shaoni leaned towards him. “Aap madad karenge?”

“Main retired hoon, beti. Mere paas ab sirf ek phone hai aur kuch purane dost.” He tapped the phone in his breast pocket. “Par purane dost kaafi hote hain. Chupchaap. Seedhe upar tak.” He stood up. “Mujhe do din do.”

I could have kissed him. I didn’t, because he was a retired Commandant and I value my life.

That’s when Golu walked up the path.

No ring light. No phone held up. He walked straight through the bells to where we were sitting, stopped in front of me, and spoke only to me.

“Professor sahab. Mujhe aapse baat karni hai.”

And Kshitij stood up.

Remember what he told me when he was fourteen. Jo log nahi sunte, unke liye likhta hoon. Baad mein proof chahiye hota hai. For ten days he had been writing proof. Every line was accurate. Every date was right. He had built the case the way a good student builds an answer, point by point, and here at last was the moment he’d been writing towards: the moment when people would have to listen.

I’ve never seen Kshitij angry before. He doesn’t shout. He opened his diary, and in a perfectly level voice he read the case against Golu, page by page, like a lawyer, looking up at him after every line.

“Day one. Tum spoons girne se pehle corridor mein the. Camera on. Day two. @ND65 ka comment tumhare live pe aaya. Day five. Tumhari gaadi neeche se aayi, usi laal mitti ke saath, jahan mujhe le gaye the. Day six. Ek budhi aurat ne tumhe Gaurav bulaya, aur tum bhaag gaye. Usi raat Rohan ko pata chal gaya hum Dronagiri gaye the. Day seven. Tumhara drone khud Rohan ke camp pe gaya, aur tum bhaag gaye. Day nine. Tum jagar mein the. Subah Rohan ko pata tha hum wahan hain. Aur tum gaayab the.”

He closed the diary.

“Tum kaun ho?”

Golu didn’t answer him. His jaw was tight. His eyes were wet again, the way they had been at the jagar.

“Main uske saath nahi hoon,” he said finally. That was all. Not an explanation. Not a defence. Just that.

“Toh phir kiske saath ho?”

Silence.

“Jao,” said Kshitij. “Please. Bas jao.”

And I (reader, I want you to know I’m not proud of this) I nodded.

Shaoni was the only one who didn’t.

“Ruko,” she said. She said it to Golu first, and then she turned to her brother. “Logic. Rohan ka message aaya: Ija ji ka khana bahut tareef laayak hai. Khana kab khaya humne? Kal dopahar. Golu kal dopahar wahan tha hi nahi. Woh raat ko jagar mein aaya. Jisne Rohan ko bataya, usne humein khana khaate dekha, ya kisi ne usse phone pe bataya ki hum khana kha rahe hain.”

Then she turned to Gabbar.

“Gabbar bhaiya. Kal dopahar aapne kisko phone kiya tha?”

Gabbar opened his mouth. And closed it. And looked at the ground.

I watched him remember it. The courtyard. The phone. Haan, khana kha rahe hain, bhatt ki churkani. I watched the thought arrive, and I watched him take it, the way you’d take a scorpion out of a child’s shoe, and throw it as far away as he could. Mahesh? Mahesh, jo cycle chalata tha? No. Not possible. Not Mahesh.

He said nothing. And that silence, I think, is the thing Gabbar Singh Negi is least proud of in his whole life.

But Kshitij had already turned away, and I had already nodded, and Golu, standing there among ten thousand bells and a thousand petitions for justice, took one last look at us and walked away down the path. He didn’t look back.

“Galti kar rahe ho,” Shaoni said quietly, to her brother and to me. “Dono.”

Neither of us answered her.

She was right. I’ll tell you now, because I’m the narrator and I’m allowed to: she was completely right, and it took me two more chapters and a very bad night in a haunted bungalow to admit it.

We had lunch at a dhaba below the temple. Bal mithai for Kshitij, who needed it. Silence for everyone else.

And then my phone started buzzing. And Shaoni’s. And Kshitij’s. And Gabbar’s. All at once, like the bells.

Kshitij got there first. He turned his screen round to us without a word.

A news app. A regional channel. A headline in big red letters:

TOURISTS STEAL “PRICELESS” EXPEDITION DOCUMENTS FROM TREKKING FIRM — POLICE ISSUE LOOKOUT

And under it, a photo: grainy, from a security camera. The Savoy’s lobby. Me, Shaoni and Kshitij checking out, with my bag over my shoulder.

The story was short. Summit Secrets, a “reputed adventure company”, had filed an FIR in Dehradun. A “so-called professor” and two associates had stolen confidential survey documents and equipment from its founder, Mr Rohan Sehgal, and were believed to be travelling in a white SUV in Kumaon. Members of the public were requested to inform the police.

Then another app. Then another. Then a WhatsApp forward from my college staff group, with seventeen shocked emojis and one message from the Head of Department that just said: Parth?

I put my phone face down on the table.

“Toh,” Kshitij said quietly, “ab police bhi.”

Outside the dhaba, a police jeep went slowly past, heading up towards the temple. The constable in the passenger seat looked at Basanti, parked in the sun, for one second longer than he needed to.

Above us in the grove, ten thousand bells moved in the wind, all speaking at once.

I hoped at least one of them was speaking for us.

For the next day and a half, we were fugitives.

I’d like to tell you we were good at it. We weren’t. Gabbar took the back roads east from Almora, through pine forest and tiny bazaars, and every time a police jeep passed us, I sank so low in my seat that my knees were in my ears. Kshitij studied me from the back seat. “Bhai. Aap Bollywood ke villain lag rahe ho.”

“Bollywood ke villain ke paas kam se kam ek helicopter hota hai,” I told him.

Gabbar didn’t laugh. He hadn’t laughed since Chitai. He drove with both hands on the wheel and his eyes on the road. When Shaoni leaned forward and tried, gently, to ask him again who he had phoned at lunch on the day of the jagar, he answered without turning his head.

“Baad mein, didi.”

His voice closed the subject like a door.

Commandant Dhami called me twice. Do din. Main kaam kar raha hoon. Phone kam use karo. So we switched our phones off and drove on towards the one person in the world who might know exactly where the cold thing was.

Gangolihat sits on a high ridge in Pithoragarh district, between the Saryu and the eastern Ramganga: a small, busy town of temples and tea shops and military pride. Ija had told us how to find Dhana ji. A flower stall on the road below the town, on the way to the caves.

She was there. A small, square woman of about sixty, wrapped in a maroon shawl, threading marigolds onto string with fingers that never stopped moving. When Gabbar told her whose son he was, she put down her string and looked at him for a long moment, and then at the rest of us.

“Ija ne bheja?” she asked him.

“Ija ne bheja.”

“Mere sasur,” she said slowly, to Shaoni. “Kundan Singh. Unke paas ek cheez thi. Ek lohe ka tukda. Mudha hua. Kehte the jab unhone usse barf mein se uthaya tha, tab woh garam tha. Bahut garam. Phir thanda ho gaya. Aur kabhi garam nahi hua.”

Underground.

Patal Bhuvaneshwar is a limestone cave about fourteen kilometres from Gangolihat. You leave the road and walk down a path through deodar forest to a small temple and a narrow hole in the hillside, where a man sits with a cash box and a cupboard of numbered lockers.

“Phone, camera, sab yahan,” he told us. “Andar kuch nahi.”

I put my phone in a locker. Kshitij put his. Shaoni put hers. Gabbar put his with the face of a man handing over a kidney.

I turned to the reader. I’m turning to you now.

“For the first time since 2009,” I said, “I am a digital orphan.”

The entrance is a narrow, steep, slippery tunnel going almost straight down, and you climb into it backwards, clutching an iron chain bolted to the rock. Eighty-odd steps into the dark, with the walls pressing close and the air getting cold and damp and old. Above you, the light shrinks to a coin and then to nothing.

Then the tunnel opens, and you are standing in another world.

The cave is about 160 metres long and, at its deepest, about ninety feet below the entrance. It isn’t one chamber but many: caves inside caves, a whole underground city carved by water over a span of time I can’t imagine. Weak electric bulbs light the way. Water drips. The rock is wet and cold and glistening, and over millions of years it has grown into shapes.

And every shape has a name.

The guide, a young man from a priestly family that has served this cave for generations, walked us through it with a small torch, and in his telling the rock came alive. That long, coiled formation on the floor: Sheshnag, the great serpent who carries the earth. Those hanging, tangled strands from the ceiling: Shiva’s matted hair. That rock with water dripping onto it: the place where Ganesha’s severed head rests. A white, lotus-shaped stone. A swan. Kamdhenu, the wish-giving cow. The four doors of the four ages of the world. Two of them are closed now, the guide told us, and two are still open.

The legend says this cave holds Shiva and all thirty-three koti gods of the Hindu world. That it was first discovered in the Treta Yuga by King Rituparna of the Sun dynasty, who followed a deer here and was guided through it by the serpent Sheshnag himself. That Adi Shankaracharya came here. And that somewhere in its depths there is a passage that runs all the way under the Himalaya to Mount Kailash.

I’m a professor. I know what limestone does with water and time. I could have stood in that cave and given a lecture on stalactites.

I didn’t. I stood in front of the stone the guide called Shiva’s own, in the cold and the silence, ninety feet under the earth, with no phone, no laptop, no Google and nothing left to check. And for the first time in eleven days, I had nothing to do but be there.

So I folded my hands.

I’ll tell you what I prayed for, because it surprised me. I didn’t pray to find the warm stone. I didn’t pray for Rohan to fail. I prayed for a boy named Deepak Rawat who liked old songs and went up a mountain and didn’t come back. And for whoever was still waiting for him.

When I opened my eyes, Kshitij was standing beside me with his diary closed and his hands folded too.

We climbed back up the chain into the last of the evening light, blinking, and the cave keeper unlocked our lockers.

I turned my phone on. Seven missed calls from Commandant Dhami. Three from my HOD. One message from Mummy: Mausi ke ghar ka khana achha hai. Tum kuch kha rahe ho?

Gabbar switched his on and stared at it, and his face went the colour of the cave walls. He walked away from us towards the trees with the phone to his ear, and I heard him say, low and furious, “Mahesh. Tu ne bataya? Sach bol—” Then he was out of earshot.

Shaoni pointed at a little shop at the top of the path. “Main paani le aati hoon,” she told me. “Sab ke liye.”

Then, passing her brother, she reached out and flicked his ear, the way she has since he was six.

“Aur tum,” she told him. “Golu se maafi maangne ki practice karo. Dil se. Numbered points mein nahi.”

That was the last thing she said.

I was reading Dhami’s messages. Kal subah. Mere log Munsiyari pahunch rahe hain. Tum log wahin aao. Kisi ko mat batana. Kshitij was writing. Gabbar was arguing with his cousin in the trees.

It took us six minutes to notice she hadn’t come back.

The little shop at the top of the path was closing. The shopkeeper told me a girl in a woollen cap had bought four bottles of water and walked back down towards the steps.

We ran down to the steps.

She wasn’t there.

The path was empty in both directions. The deodars stood dark and silent. Far below, the temple bell rang once and stopped.

On the third step from the top, lying on its side with its light still on, pointing uselessly at the forest, was her headlamp.

And beside it, neatly lined up in a row, four bottles of water.

Mussoorie.

When we got out of the car at the bottom of the ridge, in the fog, at a quarter past eleven, with every tree dripping and every shadow moving, I would not have said no to a room of release. With the diary.

It’s a long way from Champawat to Munsiyari: Pithoragarh, then north up the valleys, the road clinging to the mountains like a frightened child to its mother’s dupatta. “Nahi, bhai. Woh ek chhani tak pahunch gaya hoga.” He pointed up into the dark. “Bugyal pe darjanon chhaniyan hain. Purani pathar ki jhopdiyan. Meri purani diary mein bas itna likha tha: Khaliya, chhani, Johar aur Nanda dikhte hain. Har chhani se Johar dikhta hai.”

Three stones. The little houses villagers build for the fairies of the meadows. Every herder in Kumaon would know what that meant. Rohan’s stolen diary wouldn’t.

“Diary sab kuch nahi hai,” Kshitij said quietly. “Kuch cheezein yaad mein hoti hain.”

I’ve taught for twelve years. I have never heard a better sentence about education. I’m putting it on my office door.

Commandant Dhami’s message had said: Do log bhej raha hoon. Purane log. Chupchaap. Police subah aayegi. The two men were waiting at the trailhead in the dark: middle-aged, silent, built like door frames, with the kind of quiet watchfulness you only get from twenty years on a border. They nodded to us and said nothing. When we started up the trail by torchlight, they walked behind us, then a little to the side, and then I stopped being able to see them at all, which I assume was the point.

Khaliya Top is a long climb through rhododendron and oak forest that suddenly opens onto a huge, rolling, high meadow above the trees, at around 3,500 metres. In daylight, people say, you can see the whole Panchachuli range across the valley, five white peaks like the fingers of a hand. And far to the south-west, Nanda Devi.

At night, you can see stars, and the shapes of hills, and a fire.

There was a fire near the top. A herders’ camp: a low stone hut, a few goats penned in the lee of a wall, and an old woman sitting by the flames in a black pankhi and silver earrings, as if she had been waiting for us since the beginning of the book.

Her name was Bhaguli Amma. She was seventy-eight, she told us, and she had spent every summer of her life on this bugyal. This was her last week up here before the snow.

She gave us tea. Pahad mein, someone always gives you tea. And because it was the last night of the season, and there were strangers at her fire, and she was a pahadi grandmother and it was dark, she told us stories.

She told us about the Aanchharis.

The spirits of the high meadows, she said, spreading her hands at the dark grass around us. Up here, in the bugyals, above the trees, where the flowers grow in summer and the mist comes down without warning. Some say they’re fairies. Some say they’re the spirits of girls who died young, before they could marry, with all their wishes still unfulfilled, who never went on but stayed up here in the high places, dancing.

They love beauty. They love music. They love bright colours.

She looked straight at Shaoni’s scarf.

“Isliye, beti, upar akele mat aana. Laal kapde mat pehenna. Zor se mat gaana. Seeti mat bajana. Unko jo pasand aata hai, use woh le jaati hain.”

The British officers who wrote about these hills a hundred years ago recorded the same belief (Bhaguli Amma would be pleased to know): that the hill spirits had an anger for the colour red, and that a red thread tied at the throat could protect a person, but red clothes might draw their attention. Every valley tells it a little differently. Every grandmother is right.

Then she told us about Jeetu Bagdwal.

He is the most famous of all the Garhwali ballad heroes the fairies loved, she said: a young man of Garhwal who played the flute more beautifully than anyone had ever played. One day he went up into the high meadows and played, alone. And the Aanchharis heard him. They loved his music so much that they wanted it for themselves, forever.

So they took him.

He was never seen again. But in some villages, even now, there is a dance in his name, the Bagdwal dance, and when the drums play it, people say, Jeetu comes back for one night and dances with them.

Bhaguli Amma grinned at me across the fire. “Isliye main besura gaati hoon.”

She cackled, delighted, and the goats looked up.

I have never been so grateful to be tone-deaf. I want that on the record.

Then, softly, she told us the saddest story I heard on this whole journey: the story of the Kafal bird.

Once, she said, there was a poor widow who had only one daughter. One spring the mother went into the forest and gathered a whole basket of kafal, the small, red, sour-sweet berries that ripen on these hills in Chait. She was going to sell them at the market. It was all the money they had. She left the basket with her daughter and told her: don’t eat a single one.

When she came back that evening, the basket was less full. The berries had sunk.

She thought her daughter had eaten them. And in her anger and her fear and her poverty, she struck the girl. Too hard.

The daughter died.

That night, the dew fell. And the berries, which had only shrunk in the heat of the day, swelled back up until the basket was full again, exactly as it had been. Not one was missing.

The mother understood. And she died of grief.

Both of them became birds. Every spring in these mountains, when the kafal ripens, you can hear one bird calling, Kafal pako, maine na chakho (the kafal ripened, and I never even tasted it), and the other answering, endlessly, too late: Purai puti, purai. They’re all there, daughter. They’re all there.

Nobody said anything when she finished. Kshitij had stopped writing. Shaoni was looking into the fire. Gaurav was looking up at the stars, and I didn’t need to ask who he was thinking about.

That’s when I understood something about these stories that I’d been circling for fourteen days.

They’re not really meant to frighten you. Don’t go up alone. Don’t wander off at night. Don’t answer a voice you can’t see. Don’t follow something beautiful off the path. Don’t strike in anger; you might be wrong. Every one of them is a rule. A rule to keep you alive in the mountains, wrapped in a story so that you’ll remember it at three in the morning when you’ve forgotten everything else.

“PARTH! SWEATER PEHNA?!”

At the edge of the trees, the big orange shape on Gaurav’s screen leapt about a metre into the air.

Bhalla had spent fourteen days being frightened by ghats and red lights and walking shawls and cowbells. He had spent the last six hours listening to Bhaguli Amma describe the spirits of the meadow who take what they love. And now, in the middle of the night, the forest itself had answered him in a woman’s voice, enormous, from nowhere, demanding to know about sweaters.

He ran.

He ran downhill, straight into the trees, in one boot, crashing through the rhododendron, yelling something I will not print, because this book may be read by children and by my HOD. And he ran in exactly the direction where, I would later learn, Commandant Dhami’s two quiet men had been lying in the dark all night, waiting.

We heard the crash. A shout. A short, sharp scuffle. Then silence.

Then one of the quiet men’s voices, calm, carrying up the slope.

“Mil gaya.”

“Parth? Parth? Kya ho raha hai wahan? Kaun chilla raha hai?”

I picked up the phone. My hands were shaking so hard I nearly dropped it in the fire.

“Mummy,” I said. “Aap… aap teen baje kyun phone kar rahi ho?”

“Sapna aaya,” said my mother. “Tum thand mein baithe ho. Kaanp rahe ho. Toh maine socha phone karke pooch loon.” A suspicious pause. “Sweater pehna?”

I looked at Kshitij. Kshitij was laughing so hard he had fallen over. Shaoni had come out of the tent with her headlamp, her face going from terror to confusion to something like hysteria. Gabbar was awake and staring. Gaurav was holding the thermal camera and crying and laughing at the same time.

“Mummy,” I said, “aapne abhi ek criminal pakadwa diya.”

There was a long silence from Vadodara.

“Achha,” said my mother. “Sweater pehna?”

“Teen, Mummy. Teen.”

Nobody slept after that. We sat by the rebuilt fire and watched the sky go from black to grey to the palest, coldest blue, and waited for the light.

When it came, it came all at once: gold, flooding over the eastern ridges and lighting up the Panchachuli peaks across the valley like five enormous candles. Far to the south-west, pink and white and impossibly high, Nanda Devi caught the sun.

And on the far side of the bugyal, across a long, folded slope of frosted grass, Shaoni saw them first. She grabbed my arm and pointed.

A line of figures, moving fast. Five or six of them, headlamps still on though they didn’t need them now. Heading for a lone stone hut on a spur of the meadow that looked out over the deep valley to the north: the valley of the Gori Ganga, the old road to Johar.

In front of them, in a long dark coat, walking quickly, not looking back: Rohan.

And beside the hut, small and grey in the early light, almost invisible unless you knew what you were looking for, three round stones.

A house for the fairies.

He’d found it.

Some Questions the Mountains Keep

Day 15·Khaliya and Munsiyari

We ran.

I’d like to describe it heroically. It was a professor from the plains, a woman with a scratched face, her brother clutching a notebook, a driver in a Garhwali topi and a former YouTuber with a thermal camera, running across a frozen meadow at 3,500 metres in the first light, gasping like broken accordions.

But we ran. And behind us, coming up the trail out of the forest at a steady military jog, came Commandant Dhami’s two quiet men, and behind them, for the first time in fifteen days on our side, four police officers in khaki, their breath smoking in the cold.

The hut sat alone on its spur of meadow: low, square, built of grey stone, its slate roof half fallen in, a wooden door hanging from one hinge. Beyond it, the ground dropped away into a gulf of blue shadow: the deep valley of the Gori Ganga, the old road to Johar, Rajula’s country, winding north towards Tibet. And on the far side of the world, gold in the sunrise, Nanda Devi.

Kundan Singh had chosen his spot well. From here, his goddess could see her property.

Rohan’s men were standing outside the hut. They saw the police coming. They looked at each other and, without a word, sat down on the frosty grass and put their hands on their knees. I’ve seen students give up in exams with less dignity.

The door was open.

Rohan was inside, kneeling on the earth floor in front of a wooden box.

It wasn’t much of a box. Old wood, dark with age and smoke, the size of a small suitcase, with a rusted iron hasp. It had been under a pile of folded blankets in the corner, where a shepherd might keep his spare things. Rohan had opened it.

Inside, wrapped in a faded cloth that had once been red, lay a piece of metal.

That’s all it was. A bent, twisted bracket of dull grey metal, about the length of my forearm, with two bolt holes at one end and a jagged break at the other, as if it had been torn off something heavier. Along one side, under sixty years of grime, you could just make out a few stencilled letters in English. Not enough to read a word. Enough to know that no shepherd in Kumaon had made it.

Rohan had a small yellow device in his hand: a radiation meter. He was holding it over the bracket, very close, moving it slowly up and down.

It was silent.

He moved it again. Silent. He held it right against the metal. Silent.

He sat back on his heels and looked at it. And standing in the doorway, I watched Rohan Sehgal understand.

Behind me, Kshitij said it softly, the way you’d say a prayer. “Jo garam tha, woh barf ne liya.”

Gaurav, beside him, finished it. “Jo thanda bacha, charwaha le chala.”

The shepherds had found it in the spring of 1966: something steaming in the snow, high up near the great mountain, where nothing should be warm. They had found the box itself, I think, sunk into its own little cave of melted ice, the way the other device would be found on Nanda Kot two years later. And they had been afraid, the way any sane person would be afraid of a hot thing in the snow that the foreign climbers had told the porters not to touch. So they left it there. They left the warm heart of it to the snow, and the snow took it, deeper and deeper, just as Dorje had said on Chandrashila.

But one piece had broken off. A bracket. A fitting. Something that had held the box together, or held it down. That piece had been lying on the surface in the sun, warm from what it had been touching. And Kundan Singh, a young shepherd who couldn’t leave without proof that it had all been real, picked it up (burning his hands, perhaps), wrapped it in a red cloth and carried it away. It went cold in his hands and was never warm again.

The cold thing. Not the core. Never the core.

For sixty years, a family had guarded a harmless piece of metal as if it were the goddess’s own eye. And for years, a very clever, very rich man had chased that family’s song across the Himalaya, threatening old men and frightening mothers and sending a boy up a mountain who never came back, for this.

Rohan picked up the bracket and turned it over in his hands. Then he did something I didn’t expect.

He laughed.

Not loudly. A small, tired, cracked laugh, like something breaking very far inside him.

“A bracket,” he said, without looking up. “A bracket, Professor.”

“Ek charwahe ka sabse keemti khazana,” I told him. “Aapke liye ek bracket. Unke liye Nanda ki aankh.”

I didn’t plan to say it. It just came out.

The police came in behind me. One of the officers said Rohan’s full name, very formally, followed by the words arrest and kidnapping and criminal intimidation and a long string of sections of law that I didn’t follow, except for one phrase that finally made Rohan’s face change: radioactive material.

He stood up and brushed off his long dark coat. He didn’t resist. Even now, even here, he was polite.

As they led him out past the three round stones, one of the officers stopped by the pile of gear Rohan’s men had dropped outside the hut. Rucksacks. Ropes. The radar sledge. A stack of ice axes.

Gaurav was standing very still, looking at one of them.

It was older than the others: a wooden-shafted axe, worn smooth with use, its steel head scratched and dented by years of ice. Tied to the shaft was a laminated tag, faded by the sun: SUMMIT SECRETS — EXPEDITION EQUIPMENT.

And carved into the wood just below the head, small and careful and a little crooked, were two letters.

D.R.

“Maine kiya tha,” Gaurav said. His voice was quite calm. “Uske attharahve janamdin pe. Chaaku se. Usne mujhe daanta tha ki shaft kharab kar diya.”

Nobody moved.

He picked up the axe and turned, finally, to look at Rohan, who had stopped at the edge of the meadow between two officers.

“Company ne kaha tha Deepak crevasse mein gira. Body nahi mili. Kuch nahi mila. Kuch wapas nahi aaya.” He held up the axe. “Toh yeh kaise wapas aaya?”

Rohan looked at the axe for a long moment.

Then he said, in a quiet, almost puzzled voice, as if it still annoyed him two years later:

“He wouldn’t stop singing that song.”

That was all he said. It was enough. The officer beside him wrote it down. Commandant Dhami’s two quiet men moved, without any fuss, to stand on either side of him.

Gaurav didn’t shout. He didn’t hit him. He didn’t cry, not then. He just held his brother’s axe in both hands, very carefully, the way you’d hold something returned from the dead. He turned his back on Rohan Sehgal and looked across the valley at Nanda Devi for a long time.

I went and stood beside him. I didn’t say anything. There wasn’t anything a professor could say.

After a while, Gaurav spoke, still looking at the mountain. “Devta ne kaha tha, jo pahad ne liya hai, use wapas mat maango.”

“Haan.”

“Maine nahi maanga.” He looked down at the axe. “Pahad ne khud bhej diya.”

They took Rohan down the mountain at mid-morning. And Bhalla too, in handcuffs and one boot, who had been sitting with the quiet men since 3:16 a.m. and was, Commandant Dhami told us later, the most cooperative suspect anyone in Pithoragarh district had ever interviewed.

“Usne kaha,” the Commandant told me over the phone, “ki jungle ne usse uski maa ki awaaz mein daanta. Woh sab kuch bata dega, bas usse dobara pahad pe mat bhejo.”

Kshitij was listening on speaker. “Technically, usse kisne daanta tha?”

“Meri maa ne,” I told him.

“Toh technically woh sahi hai,” said Kshitij, and wrote it down.

Then the Commandant’s voice changed. He asked me to take the phone off speaker, and to take Gaurav aside, and to put him on. Gaurav listened for a minute. Then he held the phone out to me without a word. He asked me to stay, because, he said, he didn’t think he could hear it twice.

This is what Bhalla had told the quiet men at four in the morning, his hands in cuffs and his one bare foot wrapped in a borrowed shawl, weeping like a child.

Two years ago, high on the mountain, on the last night of the “private expedition”, Deepak had understood what the grey case was for. He told Rohan he wouldn’t go on. He said he’d go down and tell everyone. A storm was coming in. Rohan didn’t shout. Rohan was never rude. He simply said, Fine. Go down. And he kept Deepak’s rope, and Deepak’s axe, and Deepak’s radio (company equipment) and sent him down the glacier alone, at night, into the weather.

Bhalla had begged to go with him. Rohan said no. Bhalla obeyed.

All night, Bhalla said, he heard Deepak singing as he went down: the old song, his grandfather’s song, fainter and fainter in the wind. And then he didn’t hear it any more.

Pahad pe akele mat jaana.

Gaurav listened to all of it standing perfectly still, his brother’s axe in his hands, looking at the mountain. When I’d finished, he nodded, once.

“Commandant sahab ko bolna,” he told me. “Bhalla ko bolna, main usse maaf nahi kar sakta. Abhi nahi.” He swallowed. “Par usne sach bola. Deepak ke liye kisi ne toh sach bola.”

The bracket went with the police, wrapped back in its faded red cloth, along with a promise (which the officer in charge made to Gabbar, and which Gabbar made him repeat twice) that once it had been examined, it would go back to Kundan Singh’s family. Where it belonged.

We followed the others down through the rhododendron forest in the afternoon light, very slowly, saying almost nothing. My legs had stopped belonging to me somewhere around Chapter 8. I didn’t mind.

That evening, at a tea shop on the edge of Munsiyari with a view of the Panchachuli peaks turning pink in the last light, an old man with a dhol on his knee was singing to a small crowd.

Gabbar heard the first lines and stopped dead in the street.

“Rajula-Malushahi,” he told us.

So we sat down on a wooden bench with our chai, all five of us, and listened to the oldest love story in Kumaon, in the valley it came from.

I’ll tell you the bones of it, the way Gabbar whispered it to me while the old man sang. Different villages tell it differently. They always do.

Long ago, in the high valley of Johar, there lived a girl called Rajula, the daughter of a wealthy Shauka trader named Sunpati: a girl so beautiful, the song says, that the sun grew pale beside her. Far to the south, in Bairath, there was a young prince of the Katyuri kings called Malushahi. When they were both very small, their families promised them to each other.

Then the families changed their minds. Rajula’s father arranged a different marriage for her, to a powerful man from across the mountains. And Malushahi’s mother, who didn’t want a girl from the high valleys for her son, gave him a sleeping herb, so that when Rajula came looking for him, he wouldn’t wake.

And Rajula came anyway.

That’s the part the old man sang longest, his voice cracking on the high notes. A girl from Johar, alone, crossing the mountains of Kumaon on foot to find the boy she’d been promised to: through the forests, over the passes, past the temples. Rebelling against her father. Against a powerful suitor. Against the whole world that said she couldn’t.

Some say Malushahi gave up his kingdom for her and became a wandering yogi to follow her. Some say he was poisoned and brought back to life. Some say they found each other in the end, and some say they never died at all, that they’re still out there somewhere in these mountains, together.

When the old man finished, nobody clapped for a while. Then everybody did.

Shaoni was wiping her eyes and pretending she wasn’t.

“Ek ladki,” she said, to nobody in particular, “jo akele pahad paar kar gayi. Kyunki usne decide kar liya tha.”

“Tilu Rauteli jaisi,” said Gabbar.

“Didi jaisi,” said Kshitij, without looking up from his notebook.

Shaoni hit him on the arm. Then she put her head on his shoulder for exactly three seconds, which in their family is a speech.

I sat on that bench as the stars came out over Munsiyari and the Panchachuli peaks faded to grey, and I looked south-west, to where Nanda Devi stood in the dark, and I thought about everything.

About a woman who died behind a locked door in 1911. About two rivers meeting in the night at Devprayag. About a canal that cost a son and a rock that stopped a flood. About a god in five pieces and a village that never forgave Hanuman. About Greeks at a skeleton lake, and drums that wake the gods, and ten thousand bells asking for justice. About a cave where every stone is a deity, and a procession in the fog, and a goddess who agreed to take less than she asked for. About a voice in the forest that we didn’t answer.

And about a box of plutonium, somewhere in the ice of that dark mountain, still warm, still sinking, still lost.

I’m a professor. My whole life, I have believed that every question has an answer if you look hard enough. That’s what science is. That’s what code is. That’s what research is. That’s what I’ve told my students for twelve years.

The mountains don’t think so.

The mountains keep some questions on purpose. Where the device went. What the Greeks were doing at Roopkund. Who really poured the poison at the Savoy. What happens, exactly, when a god comes down into a farmer’s body at three in the morning. What the forest is, when it calls your name.

And I’m a believer. Which means I’ve finally made peace with that.

Some questions aren’t meant to be answered. Some are meant to be kept. By a grandmother at a fire. By a shepherd in a song. By a family with a red cloth and a wooden box. By a mountain.

That’s the hidden Uttarakhand, reader. That’s the untold secret of the Devbhoomi. Not one thing buried in the snow. Thousands of things, held carefully, by people who know that some stories are a responsibility before they are an answer.

My phone buzzed.

Mummy.

“Pakda gaya woh aadmi?”

“Pakda gaya, Mummy.”

“Achha hai.” A pause. “Ab ghar aa jao. Bahut ho gaya pahad-vahad.”

“Aa raha hoon.”

“Aur Parth?”

“Haan?”

“Sweater—”

“Teen, Mummy,” I said. “Hamesha teen.”

Two days later, Gabbar drove us down to Kathgodam in Basanti, slowly, with the windows open and old Garhwali songs on the stereo. Gaurav followed in his white SUV, with Deepak’s axe on the passenger seat, wrapped in a blanket.

At the railway station, Gabbar shook my hand for a long time.

“Yahan se seedha Ija ke paas,” he told me. “Ek hafta. Phir har mahine. Season ho ya na ho.”

He looked faintly embarrassed, the way men his age look when they’ve made a decision out loud.

“Unke saare geet record karne hain. Gaurav ne phone mein app daal diya hai. Is baar sununga, Professor sahab. Neend mein nahi. Jaag ke.”

Shaoni, who had organised the app, the recording schedule and a shared folder named Ija — Songs — FINAL, looked enormously pleased with herself.

Gabbar was still holding my hand. “Professor sahab. Agli chhutti mein kahan?”

I turned to the reader. I’m turning to you now.

“Gabbar bhai,” I said, “jahan network ho.”

Epilogue

Six Months Later

In April, the trial of Rohan Sehgal began in Dehradun.

I’m not going to tell you much about it, because it isn’t finished, and because courtrooms are not mountains — they don’t keep secrets as well, but they’re much slower about giving them up. Summit Secrets has been shut down. The charges are long. Some of them have words in them I had to look up. And among them, now, is the case of a young high-altitude porter from Dronagiri named Deepak Rawat, which has been formally reopened.

Bhalla is a witness. He has asked, through his lawyer, never to be sent above 2,000 metres again. The court has been sympathetic. His real name, it turns out, is Bhuwan Singh. Everyone has called him Bhalla — the spear — since he was the tallest boy in his school, in a village outside Pithoragarh, exactly as Gabbar guessed. In March he wrote a letter to Deepak’s mother. Gaurav hasn’t let her read it yet. He has read it himself, he told me, eleven times.

Mahesh is a witness too. Gabbar hasn’t spoken to him yet. Ija has, and from what Gabbar tells me, it was the most frightening hearing of the whole case.

The bracket came back in February.

The experts confirmed what the radiation meter had said on the meadow: an old piece of metal mounting hardware, almost certainly from the 1965 expedition equipment. Harmless. Cold. Sixty years old. They returned it to Dhana ji in Gangolihat, wrapped in its faded red cloth.

In May, when the snow cleared, she carried it back up to Khaliya, to Kundan Singh’s hut, and put it back in the wooden box under the blankets. Then she rebuilt the three-stone house for the Aanchharis beside the door, which had been knocked over in all the fuss. She sent me a photograph. There are fresh flowers on the stones.

The Nanda Devi device has not been found.

As I write this, it is still somewhere in the ice of that mountain. Still, perhaps, a little warm. Every summer, the glaciers are a little smaller. Every summer, somebody in the villages below asks the question again. I hope, one day, someone who should find it finds it — carefully, safely, with every precaution in the world.

And until then, I hope the mountain keeps it.

Gaurav Rawat has a new channel.

It isn’t called Ghostbuster Golu. The old channel is still there, all five million subscribers and four hundred ghost videos, frozen like a museum. The last thing on it is a short clip of a thermal camera at 3:14 in the morning on Khaliya Top, and a woman’s enormous voice demanding to know about sweaters. It has more views than anything I will ever do in my life.

The new channel is just his name. Gaurav Rawat — Pahad Ki Kahaniyan. No ring light. No capital letters. He wears the old blue cap in every episode. And he still ends every video the same way, looking straight into the lens — but quietly now, without the wink:

“Pahad pe akele mat jaana.”

He walks up to the high villages with a camera, and he asks the grandmothers and the jagariyas and the shepherds to tell him their stories, and he films them telling it, in their own words, in their own languages, before the stories go quiet. Bachi Ram ji was his first episode. Kamla Devi was his second. She told the Hanuman story again, and laughed at the end, and the comments were full of people from all over India saying we never knew.

In Dronagiri, there is a small stone now, at the edge of the village, facing the mountain. It has Deepak’s name on it, and the first lines of his grandfather’s song. His ice axe hangs in Gaurav’s house, with the crooked D.R. still carved into the shaft.

I was the new channel’s first subscriber. He still credits me, in every video, as Unknown Entity (Professor?). I’ve stopped arguing.

I went back to college in November.

My students had all seen the video. All of them. Within one week, every single one was calling me Unknown Entity Sir. The HOD called me into his office, closed the door, looked at me for a long time over his glasses, and said, “Parth. Next time you want to go on holiday — Goa.” Then he asked me to give a guest lecture on the Nanda Devi expedition to the entire department. It was the best-attended lecture of my career. Nobody asked for an extension.

The little brass Hanuman on my desk faces forward now. Always. I told him about Dronagiri, quietly, one evening when the staff room was empty. I’m fairly sure he understood. He’s had a lot of practice being loved in one valley and blamed in the next.

And on my office door, there’s a sheet of paper, laminated, in Kshitij’s handwriting:

Diary sab kuch nahi hai. Kuch cheezein yaad mein hoti hain. — K.

He made me credit him. I said I would. I did.

Shaoni has started trekking.

Properly. With a spreadsheet. She’s walked to Kedarkantha and Har Ki Dun and Brahmatal, always with a group, always with a guide, always, as she tells me firmly, following every single rule the grandmothers taught us. She says it’s logic. Gabbar says it’s Tilu Rauteli. Her red scarf stays in her bag above the treeline.

Kshitij has filled four more diaries. He’s writing something. He won’t tell me what it is. He says I’ll find out when it’s published, and that I’ll be in it, and that I should prepare myself emotionally.

Gabbar still drives Basanti, but only three weeks a month. The fourth week, he’s at Ija’s, recording songs into a phone, being smacked on the head for forgetting the words. He has a new sticker on the back windshield, under the pink vinyl letters, that Gaurav made for him. It says: BHOOTON KA EXTRA.

And my mother has added a new question to her calls. It comes right after Sweater pehna?

It is: “Koi gunda toh nahi hai na aas-paas?”

This August, in the late summer of 2027, the Nanda Devi Raj Jat set out from Nauti.

The twelve-year pilgrimage. The goddess Nanda carried from her parents’ home to her husband’s, on a three-week walk through the high Himalaya, led by a four-horned ram. Whatever had been argued about the year before, this time the doli moved, and the valley moved with it.

We walked part of it. All of us. Gabbar, with Ija on his arm for the first few kilometres. Gaurav, filming, quietly, from the back. Shaoni, with her spreadsheet. Kshitij, writing. And me.

I didn’t ask any questions. I didn’t take any notes. I didn’t open my laptop once.

For three days, I just walked behind a goddess and a ram with four horns, among thousands of people singing, through meadows where the flowers were out and the Aanchharis were presumably dancing, past a lake full of old bones where everyone fell silent, towards a mountain that keeps its secrets.

And for once in my life, I didn’t need an answer.

I was a believer. That was enough.

The week after I came home, there was a parcel on my desk at college. Police seal on it. A letter from Dehradun. My own notebook — the one Rohan took from me at the Savoy, the one with Sir, likhte rehna stamped inside the cover — released from evidence and returned.

Every page was there. The missing page with my mother’s number had been found in Rohan’s coat and taped carefully back in place.

I flipped to the last page, because I am a professor and I always check the last page first.

Under my own last entry, in the careful, elegant handwriting I’d first seen in a corridor at the Savoy, there was one new line. Written, I suppose, in some quiet moment between his arrest and his trial, by a man who still couldn’t stop himself.

Nanda Kot, 1967. Ek aur geet hai. — R.

I sat with it for a long time.

Then I closed the notebook, and put it in the drawer, and locked the drawer. And I sat there in my office, with the laminated sentence on the door and the brass Hanuman facing forward, and I did not google Nanda Kot 1967.

For almost an hour.

That night, Kshitij sent me a photo on WhatsApp.

A brand-new diary. Brown cover. Still in its plastic.

Underneath, a message:

Bhai. Mummy aunty se poochna — sweater kitne hain?

And then, a second photo. The first page of the new diary, already open, already written on, in his small, careful hand.

About the Author

By day, Prof. Parth D. Joshi teaches young minds how machines think. By night, he writes code while the house sleeps. And somewhere in between, he collects stories — of gods, ghosts, and the places where the two meet.

For twelve years he has been an Assistant Professor at L. J. College of Computer Applications (LJCCA), Ahmedabad, where his students know him as the professor who answers a doubt with a story. He is the founder and mentor of CodeSprint Labs and AiRA Lab.

A believer who codes, he lights a diya every evening, has never once passed a mandir without folding his hands — and still has not googled Nanda Kot 1967.

He is also the author of Ramayana Reimagined: The Eternal Legacy, available in more than 150 countries, and of several academic books. Hidden Uttarakhand is his latest.
